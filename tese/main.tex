\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction} %DONE
Edge web services that encompass monitoring, auto-scaling and routing must
coordinate these functions across the boundaries between them. 
How well such a service absorbs a
change in demand therefore depends not only on what each function does
individually, but also on the interfaces between them. Coordination across those
interfaces can fall short, and where it does, orchestration efficiency and
reliability can degrade and may reduce the quality of service users
experience~\parencite{Bogdanov2018Kurma,Podolskiy2018IaaSReactiveAutoscalingPerformanceChallenges}.

The remainder of this chapter is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation} presents the context, motivation and
problem statement; Section~\ref{sec:objectives} states the objectives;
Section~\ref{sec:research_questions} lists the research questions;
Section~\ref{sec:research_methodology} details the methodology used to
develop the thesis; Section~\ref{sec:contributions} summarises the main
contributions; and Section~\ref{sec:document_structure} outlines the rest
of the dissertation.

\section{Context, Motivation, and Problem Statement}\label{sec:context_motivation}
In 2025, global internet traffic across fixed and mobile networks exceeded
7 000 exabytes\footnote{One exabyte equals \(10^{9}\) gigabytes.}, more than
doubling since 2020~\parencite{ITU2025InternetTraffic}, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:internet_traffic}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/global-internet-traffic-over-the-years.pdf}
    \caption{Global internet traffic between 2020 and 2025. Source: Statista, based on ITU data~\parencite{ITU2025InternetTraffic}.}
    \label{fig:internet_traffic}
\end{figure}

    This growth accompanies the emergence of real-time interactive and
data-intensive applications. Examples include web streaming
and cloud gaming, both services where users expect sub-second latency and
high-resolution multimedia, highlighting the need for fast and capable
data processing.

Cloud computing has been the backbone that enables these applications to
operate at scale. It offers automatic scaling, both horizontally
and vertically, a pay-per-use model and, among other aspects, a
distributed offering and availability that have established it as the
foundational layer supporting modern applications and services
\parencite{Gurung2026CloudRevolutionTracingOriginsRise}.

    Yet, in parallel, a growing share of internet traffic now originates from
mobile devices, which have become the main gateway to internet access
\parencite{Gaudiaut2026MobileTrafficShare}. Much of this access is
therefore generated and consumed at the network edge. Edge computing
brings computational and storage resources closer to the end users,
allowing for much lower latency in delivering services and
applications~\parencite{Cao2020OverviewEdgeComputingResearch,Satyanarayanan2017EmergenceEdgeComputing}.
Since data can be processed and stored locally, only a subset of it is
sent to the cloud~\parencite{Cao2020OverviewEdgeComputingResearch}. 

    Despite these advantages, edge resources are dispersed, which translates
into less capacity per location and greater complexity in managing the
distributed edge servers. As Satyanarayanan puts it, \enquote{the dispersion inherent in
edge computing raises the complexity of management considerably}
\parencite[p.~37]{Satyanarayanan2017EmergenceEdgeComputing}. This complexity is
aggravated by considerable variation in user demand over time and by the
heterogeneous resource requirements of the requests served. Luo et al. further
highlight that, without efficient resource scheduling, this distributed nature
leads to resource waste
\parencite{Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}. Resource
management therefore becomes increasingly important, since the limited
capacity of each location must be adjusted over time to suit the constant
fluctuation of user demand.

Scale-up alone does not guarantee usable capacity instantly. The
orchestration must detect the demand shift, deliver that evidence to the
decision point, provision the right kind of capacity, and admit traffic to it,
and every step hands over to the next across a boundary between functions.
This sequence is the demand-to-capacity chain, the path that converts a change
in overall user demand into capacity that serves that demand: monitoring and
telemetry observe the shift, a decision point selects a scaling action, the
action provisions new capacity, and that capacity reaches a readiness state
(it can answer requests, not merely run) and starts receiving traffic, which
is the point at which it becomes usable.\footnote{An \emph{interface} is a
boundary between two steps of the chain at which two functions meet; what
crosses it is the evidence, the command, or the state the next step acts on,
and a \emph{handoff delay} is the latency between a function's handover and
the next step acting on it. The reviewed literature addresses these
boundaries mainly between functions that run under independent control loops,
each observing, deciding, and acting on its own. The \emph{coordination gap}
is the accumulated effect along the chain, which this dissertation does not
measure as a whole.} The three interfaces between its steps, highlighted in
Figure~\ref{fig:demand_capacity_chain}, are the subject of this thesis.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/src/demand_to_capacity_chain.drawio.png}
    \caption{The demand-to-capacity chain: from a demand shift to usable
    capacity, with the three interfaces studied in this dissertation
    highlighted.}
    \label{fig:demand_capacity_chain}
\end{figure}

The steps described above must be coordinated under the time pressure of
an ongoing demand shift. They may run as separate components, each with its
own control loop, or within one control process, and the interfaces between
them remain in both arrangements. In the reviewed literature, the cost of these crossings is
acknowledged but never isolated or varied as an independent
variable~\parencite{Qu2018AutoScalingWebApplicationsClouds,
Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.
The crossings also differ in more than latency: telemetry can arrive
complete and fresh, complete but delayed, or with summaries
missing~\parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork};
a scaling action can serve the tier that binds the demand or one that
does not~\parencite{Nikravesh2017ImpactDatabaseLayerAutoScaling}; and the
state that admits a backend to traffic can be late or 
inaccurate~\parencite{Dreibholz2023TowardsLightweightTaskSchedulingFramework}.

    This thesis isolates the three interfaces of the chain: how telemetry is
consumed by the controller (the telemetry mode); whether the scaling action
serves the tier the demand presses on (bottleneck-aware scaling action); and
whether a ready backend is admitted on the backend's notification alone, on
the notification with a fallback check, through periodic polling, or on the
notification with a confirmation check (readiness admission). The platform is
organised as two peer \ac{SDN} controllers, one per network domain, and the
consuming functions of the chain (telemetry consumption, scaling decisions,
and traffic routing) run in the same controller process, so that each
interface can be configured and varied on its own rather than observed as an
incidental coupling. Telemetry production and aggregation remain external to
the controller, so how aggregated telemetry is consumed is an explicit
boundary, while scaling and admission are exposed as lifecycle hooks.

    The thesis does not claim that this SDN-based architecture is superior
to existing orchestration platforms such as Kubernetes or NFV Management
and Orchestration (MANO) platforms, nor that its mechanisms generalise
beyond the tested infrastructure. It claims only that these three interfaces 
are measurable in isolation and that measurement can establish which property
of each interface decides the outcome, and under what conditions. The reviewed
literature has not previously characterised them this way, and no
aggregate cost along the chain is claimed.

\section{Objectives}\label{sec:objectives} %DONE
The main objective of this thesis is to contribute to more efficient
resource orchestration for edge services by characterising the
interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain under controlled demand regimes,
and by establishing, for each interface, which property of the crossing
decides the outcome and under which conditions: how telemetry is consumed by the
controller, whether the scaling action serves the tier the
demand presses on, and whether a ready backend is admitted on its
notification alone, on the notification with a fallback check, through
periodic polling, or on the notification with a confirmation check.

    The platform keeps controller-side coordination in-process, with how
telemetry is consumed by the controller retained as an explicit, configurable
boundary. Along with explicit lifecycle hooks for scaling and admission, this arrangement
exposes the studied interfaces to direct measurement. Because the
components share one process and are therefore coupled, making each
interface independently configurable is itself a deliberate design choice,
so that the platform can vary the telemetry mode, the scaling
action taken, or the admission of ready capacity, and measure the effect.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:research_questions} %DONE
This thesis seeks to answer the following three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{RQ1}: How do the four telemetry modes (push, delayed
    push, polling, and sampled push) affect how the controller scales the
    service when load rises?

    \item \textit{RQ2}: When load presses on the compute tier or on the
    storage tier, how does scaling whichever is under pressure, rather than
    always scaling compute or always scaling storage, affect service
    recovery and resource use?

    \item \textit{RQ3}: When a new backend is provisioned,
    how do the four admission modes (backend notification, notification
    with a fallback check, periodic polling, and notification with a
    confirmation check) affect whether and when the new
    capacity starts serving requests and what users experience, under
    three message conditions: the message arrives intact, never arrives,
    or arrives before the backend can serve?
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Methodology}\label{sec:research_methodology} %DONE
The work presented in this dissertation follows the Design Science Research
(DSR) methodology \parencite{Hevner2004DesignScienceInformationSystemsResearch1},
\parencite{vomBrocke2020IntroductionDesignScienceResearch}
proposed in \parencite{Peffers2007DesignScienceResearchMethodologyInformation},
and demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:design_science_research_methodology}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/Design Science Research process model.png}
    \caption{Design Science Research Methodology}
    \label{fig:design_science_research_methodology}    
\end{figure}

    Design science produces and evaluates purposeful artefacts that address certain
real-world problems, the research contribution corresponds to the knowledge gained
by designing the artefact and evaluating it against defined objectives.
In \ac{DSRM} the research is organised into six categories: problem
identification and motivation, definition of the objectives of a solution,
design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication.

    This dissertation follows a problem-centred approach, in which the problem
motivates the research and is defined as the coordination between the
functions that orchestrate an edge service, where handovers that are late,
incomplete, mismatched, or unverified can lower orchestration efficiency
and the quality of service users experience. The problem definition is grounded in the
state of the art reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}. The six
activities unfolded as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Problem identification and motivation.} Edge services whose
monitoring, scaling and routing modules operate as independent components
depend on information that crosses the boundaries between them, and how
those crossings are operated can degrade orchestration
efficiency. Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation} introduces the problem, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review} establishes that these functions are
typically studied in isolation, which motivates and delimits it.

\item \textit{Objectives of a solution.} As defined in Section~\ref{sec:objectives},
the solution is an experimental platform, not a novel algorithm, whose
purpose is to make the interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain
measurable.

\item \textit{Design and development.} The artefact is the single-process platform
described in Chapters~\ref{ch:system_architecture} and~\ref{ch:implementation}.
It is built from two peer SDN controllers (OS-Ken/OpenFlow), one per network
domain, each hosting telemetry consumption, elasticity decision-making and the
double-VIP routing model, with Docker containerised service backends and a
MongoDB replica set per domain as the storage tier, scaled through native
replica-set membership.

\item \textit{Demonstration.} The artefact was deployed in an emulated
two-domain edge environment and driven with scheduled workload phases
reproducing demand shifts, demonstrating the chain end to end from demand
observation through scaling action to traffic admission, with the tested
interface varied across runs.

\item \textit{Evaluation.} Each research question was evaluated in a dedicated
experimental campaign, under a pre-registered measurement and statistical
procedure, reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. A campaign is the set of runs
that answers one research question by varying one interface while the others
are held fixed, and each run executes the campaign's workload as an
experimental unit. The workloads are issued by a scheduled, open-loop driver
that applies the offered request schedule independently of the responses it
receives, so the offered load is preserved when the service degrades. The
primary comparisons are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}, and
the threats to validity are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\item \textit{Communication.} The problem, the artefact and the findings are
communicated through this dissertation, its public defence, and any resulting
publications.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Main Contributions of this Dissertation}\label{sec:contributions} %DONE
This dissertation makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item An experimental characterisation of four telemetry modes (push,
    delayed push, polling, and sampled push) and of how they affect how the
    controller scales the service when load rises (RQ1).
    \item An experimental characterisation of bottleneck-aware scaling
    action selection, more specifically how deciding between
    compute and storage scale-up
    from the observed bottleneck, compared with fixed single-tier policies,
    affects targeted-tier relief, service recovery, and resource use under
    a mixed press and a data-access press (RQ2).
    \item An experimental characterisation of readiness admission: how the
    four admission modes affect whether and when new capacity starts serving
    requests, under three message conditions (RQ3).
    \item A comparative synthesis of the three interfaces studied
    (telemetry modes, scaling action, and readiness admission)
    under their respective controlled workload regimes, identifying which
    effects were robust, conditional, or not established.
\end{enumerate}

    These contributions are claimed within the evaluated two-domain testbed
and workload families.

\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:literature_review} %DONE
This chapter presents the background and related work that motivated
the research, selected through the search strategy described in
Section~\ref{sec:review_method}. The review is separated into two parts.
First, the technologies and concepts used to build the experimental
platform are explained and surveyed, each introduced for what it is
and why it suits the edge scenario. These are, more specifically,
edge computing, SDN and OpenFlow, containerisation, and NoSQL
document stores with a focus on MongoDB.

    On top of these technologies runs resource orchestration, the layer that
joins monitoring and telemetry, auto-scaling, and load balancing into one
service. The review then focuses on the three interfaces of the
demand-to-capacity chain: how a load rise is detected, how the scaling action
is chosen when several are available, and how new capacity is admitted to
traffic.

    For each interface the review asks the same two questions: does the
reviewed work vary this interface, and does it measure what happens
downstream? This grounds the claim of Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}:
the literature acknowledges these interfaces but has not studied them one at
a time. Section~\ref{sec:lit_sdn_orchestration} reviews the platforms these
functions run on, and Section~\ref{sec:lit_synthesis} collects the gaps.

\section{Platform Technology Background}\label{sec:platform_background} %DONE
This section presents the four building blocks of the evaluated testbed, edge
computing, SDN with OpenFlow, containerisation, and MongoDB, each stating what it
is and why it matters for the thesis scenario. How they are applied is the
subject of Chapters~\ref{ch:system_architecture} and~\ref{ch:implementation}.

\subsection{Edge Computing} %DONE
Edge computing places computational and storage resources between end devices
and centralised cloud data centres, close to where data is produced and
consumed. It complements rather than replaces the cloud as latency-sensitive
processing and storage move to the edge, while large-scale analytics and
long-term storage remain
centralised~\parencite{Cao2020OverviewEdgeComputingResearch}. Processing and
storing data locally lowers the latency of interactive services and reduces the
ingress bandwidth into the cloud
\parencite{Satyanarayanan2017EmergenceEdgeComputing}.

    The dispersion of edge resources also raises management complexity. Resource
heterogeneity, scarcity, and demand varying over time can all degrade
orchestration efficiency, and scheduling that does not follow the workload leads
either to over-provisioning, where resources are held but not used, or to
under-provisioning, where service quality
degrades~\parencite{Liu2019SurveyEdgeComputingSystemsTools,Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}.
The joint scheduling of communication, computing, and storage remains an open
direction~\parencite{Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}, and the
combination of edge platforms with SDN is recorded as an area with room for
further investigation~\parencite{Nain2024ResourceOptimizationEdgeSDNEdge}. The
service evaluated in this dissertation runs in an emulated edge environment as an
edge service whose capacity must follow fluctuating demand, which is the
management problem the remainder of this chapter reviews.

\subsection{SDN and OpenFlow} %DONE
Software-Defined Networking separates the control plane, which decides
how traffic is handled, from the data plane, which forwards packets, so
that forwarding behaviour can be reprogrammed centrally from a
controller instead of being configured device by device. 

Baktir et al. survey how edge computing benefits from this decoupling,
arguing that SDN lowers the complexity barriers and enhances the flexibility of
edge deployments \parencite{Baktir2017HowCanEdgeComputingBenefit}.

The OpenFlow protocol implements the decoupling through match-action
flow rules installed on the switches, and is, as reviewed by Waseem et al.,
the most extensively used SDN protocol in the research community
\parencite{Waseem2022SoftwareDefinedNetworkingSDNReview}. 

OpenFlow fixes the header fields a switch can match and rewrite by standard,
and new fields arrive with new versions of the protocol, whereas a language such
as P4 lets the operator write the program that defines how packets are parsed
and processed \parencite{Liatifis2023AdvancingSDNOpenFlowP4Survey}.

In its OpenFlow form, a single controller re-programs the forwarding
behaviour of the network by installing and updating match-action flow rules,
so that network behaviour can follow the life cycle of the services it
carries without reconfiguring the forwarding devices individually.
In this thesis, that programmability keeps the routing decisions inside
the platform's control loop: which backend serves a request, when newly
scaled capacity joins the traffic path, and which storage member serves a
read are all decided by the controller and enacted as flow rules. The
decoupling is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sdn_architecture}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/src/sdn_architecture.drawio.png}
    \caption{Software-Defined Networking architecture: the control plane is
    decoupled from the data plane and programs the forwarding devices through
    the OpenFlow protocol.}
    \label{fig:sdn_architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Containerisation and Docker} %DONE
Containers package an application with its dependencies and share the
host kernel, which makes them lightweight and quick to start up compared
to virtual machines, properties that suit resource-constrained edge
platforms. In this thesis Docker is adopted as the platform's
containerisation tool because of its wide adoption for building and
running containers.

\subsection{NoSQL and MongoDB}\label{sec:platform_storage} %DONE
Document-oriented NoSQL stores hold schema-flexible documents rather than rows
of relational tables. Because related data is embedded in one document and the
model avoids joins, records can be partitioned and replicated across servers,
which is how these stores scale horizontally, whereas relational deployments
have traditionally grown by enlarging a single server. The model suits the
heterogeneous data of edge and IoT services, whose structure varies across
sources and over time.

MongoDB is a document store of this family, holding data as BSON documents
within collections. Redundancy is organised natively around the replica set, a
group of \texttt{mongod} processes that maintain the same data set. One member is
elected primary and serves the clients' writes, recording them in an operation
log, which the remaining members, the secondaries, apply asynchronously.
Members exchange heartbeats, and when the primary fails or becomes unreachable
the secondaries hold an election, so the set keeps serving without operator
intervention~\parencite{MongoDBDocsReplicaSet}. Members can also be added and
removed while the set keeps serving, which turns the replica set into a
controllable unit of storage capacity rather than a fixed configuration. Figure~\ref{fig:mongodb_replica_set} shows the
members of a set and the replication paths between them.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/src/mongodb_replica_set.drawio.png}
    \caption{MongoDB replica set: a primary member serving the clients and
    secondary members to which the data is replicated.}
    \label{fig:mongodb_replica_set}
\end{figure}

    This dissertation builds on that capability. The storage tier is
scaled by adding and removing replica-set members while the set keeps
serving, leveraging MongoDB's native replication.

\section{Telemetry}\label{sec:lit_monitoring} % DONE
Monitoring collects the resource and load metrics of a running service, and
telemetry carries them to the controller. Every scaling decision is taken from
that telemetry, so when it is late or incomplete the reaction is late or based
on an incomplete picture, and in an edge deployment the metrics are produced
across several nodes before they reach the decision point. How telemetry arrives
is therefore the first interface studied in this thesis.

    Telemetry is delivered in one of two common patterns: event-driven, where
the producer sends each summary as it closes, and polling, where the consumer
reads the latest summary at a fixed interval. Either pattern can reach the
consumer late, or with summaries missing, according to how it is configured:
a read at the same interval as the summaries delivers every summary, at most one period
old, while a slower read, or a producer that samples or holds summaries back,
loses or delays them. The four telemetry modes compared in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are these two defects separately and together: push
(every summary, on time), delayed push (every summary, 30~s late), sampled
push (one summary in three, on time), and polling (only the latest summary at
each 30~s read, so it is late and misses two in three).

    Monitoring is conventionally pull-based, with the consumer requesting
the latest sample on a fixed interval
\parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork}. In orchestrators this is a periodic
scrape into a time-series database, as in OSM-based NFV, where the collector
polls the infrastructure into Prometheus and the control modules read it
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}. The
reviewed work that comes closest to the delivery question is AdapPF, which
varies the Prometheus scrape interval in a Kubernetes federation and shows
that a coarse sixty-second interval degrades scheduling accuracy relative
to a five-second one, while an adaptive interval restores it and reduces
monitoring traffic
\parencite{Huang2023AdapPFSelfAdaptiveScrapeInterval}. Yaseen records the
\enquote{visibility gaps} that polling leaves between reads, and calls for monitoring
tightly integrated with control
\parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork}. Hung et al.
show that telemetry collection and routing can run at one controller, but
their loop does not scale the service
\parencite{Hung2022SDNControllerMonitoringArchitecture5G}. Usman et al. leave
the choice of measurement interval open
\parencite{Usman2022SurveyObservabilityDistributedEdgeAmp}. Zhou and Yong
change what is monitored, from CPU load to application errors, and scaling
quality follows the metric, but the delivery itself is not varied, and the
finding comes from a single small testbed
\parencite{Zhou2024ImplementHPANginxServiceUsing}.

    Within the reviewed literature, no work separates late telemetry from
missing telemetry, and none follows the effect of a telemetry mode through the
service to what its users experience. AdapPF comes closest: it
varies freshness and measures a downstream consequence, but its comparison
does not separate late from missing updates. Separating delay from loss, and
measuring both through the service, is the gap this dissertation fills.

\section{Auto Scaling}\label{sec:lit_auto_scaling} %DONE
Auto-scaling adjusts the resources allocated to a service to the load it
receives, capacity is provisioned as demand grows and released as it recedes, so
the service keeps serving without holding resources it does not
use. Strategies are mostly grouped into horizontal scaling, which changes the
number of units, and vertical scaling, which changes the capacity of a single
unit, with hybrid strategies combining the
two~\parencite{ElMrabet2025ComprehensiveSurveyTaxonomyAutoscalingTechniques}.
Horizontal scaling suits the edge scenario and is the direction this dissertation
follow where adding a unit of capacity to a tier is scale-up, and removing one is
scale-down.

In edge computing a site's capacity is bounded by the nodes deployed there,
so enlarging an individual unit has a hard ceiling, whereas adjusting the number
of units follows demand. When and how many resources to add or remove therefore
determines both the quality of service during a demand shift and the efficiency
with which scarce resources are managed.

Qu et al. classify the auto-scalers along several characteristics,
among them the scaling indicator, the resource estimation method, and
the timing of the action, and Taherizadeh and Stankovski present a
taxonomy for edge computing whose dimensions include adaptation
intervals and architectural support for multi-tier applications, noting
that a scaling approach should account for different dynamic
provisioning policies at each tier. Both surveys take the
scaling of service instances, whether virtual machines or containers,
as the unit of analysis. The main questions the field asks are when to
scale and how many instances to add, which kind of capacity is
provisioned, when applicable, is not among the classification dimensions
\parencite{Qu2018AutoScalingWebApplicationsClouds,Taherizadeh2017AutoScalingApplicationsEdgeComputing}.

With SDN, the traffic side is added, but the scaled
resource remains the one the service function is built from.
Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. couple SDN-based traffic steering with
horizontal scaling of virtualised network functions, so that the SDN
controllers re-program the forwarding paths while instances of the same
pre-dimensioned function pool are activated or deactivated. The decision
reduces to whether and how many instances of that one function to
activate.\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.
In such a service, however, the storage tier is
itself a live capacity. When the pressure sits in the data rather than
in the function instances, adding instances does not relieve the storage tier.

Predictive and learning-based approaches sharpen the same decision
without widening it. Toka et al. formalise the Kubernetes horizontal
autoscaler's control model to anticipate demand for edge clusters
\parencite{Toka2021MachineLearningScalingManagementKubernetes}, Ju et
al. and Cheng et al. forecast workload to adjust replica counts ahead of
demand, the latter jointly with placement
\parencite{Ju2021ProactiveAutoscalingEdgeComputingSystems,Cheng2023ProScaleProactiveAutoscalingMicroserviceTime}.
Across these systems the optimisation lies on the forecast and on adjusting the
number of compute replicas over time. The improvement pursued is a better
forecast of how many instances to run, and which tier holds the limiting
resource does not enter the scope of study.

Scaling one resource does not always make the service better,
because the pressure may sit elsewhere. Nikravesh et al. quantify what
that default costs in a three-tier web service. The scaling decisions
taken for the business tier turn out to be insensitive to database
capacity, yet reducing database capacity raises the rate of SLA
violations, so service quality can be governed by the tier that the
scaling decisions ignore
\parencite{Nikravesh2017ImpactDatabaseLayerAutoScaling}. Dogani et
al. make the same observation in their survey, noting that adding
resources does not necessarily raise throughput when the bottleneck
lies elsewhere \parencite{Dogani2023AutoScalingTechniquesContainerCloud}. 
Among the systems covered by that survey, only a few take cross-service effects
into account. Waterfall, a machine-learning auto-scaler, accounts
for how scaling one microservice can shift the bottleneck towards
downstream services.

PBScaler identifies the microservice whose degradation
propagates through the invocation graph, ranks candidate
bottlenecks, and optimises replica management around the
identified bottleneck
\parencite{Xie2024PBScalerBottleneckAwareAutoscalingFramework}.
Bottleneck awareness therefore exists in the reviewed state of the art,
but the bottleneck it locates is always a microservice and the action it
takes is always replica scaling, so the choice between
compute and storage capacity remains outside the action space.

The closest reviewed precedent to choosing between resource tiers
is the provisioning model of Li et al., which adds both cloud compute
instances and data replicas in cloud-assisted edge systems, from a
forecast of future workload built over historical workload data
\parencite{Li2020HeterogeneityAwareElasticProvisioningCloud}. Both
actions are available, but the trigger is a forecast rather than a live
observed bottleneck, and the capacity is provisioned across edge hosts
and rented cloud instances, with the outcome measured in tenancy cost. 

Overall, the literature shows that the kind of capacity added matters,
compute replicas do not relieve pressure whose limiting resource lies in the
storage tier, and database capacity can govern SLA violations while a
compute-tier scaling decision remains unchanged. Scaling actions are therefore
not interchangeable merely because each adds resources. In the reviewed
studies, which kind of capacity an action adds remains an architectural given,
where a second kind is available, as in Li et al., a forecast settles the choice,
while runtime bottleneck identification, as in PBScaler and Waterfall, still acts
on compute replicas only. This dissertation isolates that choice, with storage
scale-up among the actions the controller may take at runtime, and measures the
resulting differences in targeted-tier relief, service recovery, and resource
use.

\section{Load Balancing and Readiness Admission on SDN}\label{sec:lit_sdn_lb} %DONE
Load balancing distributes incoming traffic among a set of available
backends so that no single backend is overloaded while others remain
underutilised. In SDN networks this distribution is programmed centrally,
where the controller installs forwarding rules that steer each flow to a
selected backend, and a load-balancing policy decides which backend
receives the next flow, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:sdn_load_balancing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/src/sdn_load_balancing.drawio.png}
    \caption{Load balancing in an SDN network: the controller installs
    forwarding rules that distribute client traffic among the available
    backends.}
    \label{fig:sdn_load_balancing}
\end{figure}

    Load balancing is a common application of SDN. Belgaum et al. survey
the field systematically and group its techniques into conventional and
AI-based families
\parencite{Belgaum2020SystematicReviewLoadBalancingTechniques}.
Neghabi et al. classify the mechanisms as static and dynamic, and observe
that controller state can fall out of step between synchronizations
\parencite{Neghabi2018LoadBalancingMechanismsSoftwareDefined}. Abdelltif
et al. place the balancer on the controller itself, holding per-server
load current over the OpenFlow pool
\parencite{Abdelltif2018SDNLoadBalancingServiceCloud}.
Taken together, these studies treat load balancing as a selection
problem. At a given routing decision, the policy decides which backend
receives the next flow, choosing by criteria such as load, path
properties, and resource utilisation. The set of backends receiving
traffic is taken as given.

    The membership of that set, however, is not fixed. Backends are
added as demand rises and removed as it recedes. Capacity becomes usable
only when traffic can reach it. A scaling action provisions a backend,
but the provisioned backend serves nothing until it is admitted to the
set of backends receiving traffic. The scaling loop that began with the
demand shift closes at that admission, and the time a ready backend
spends outside the set is therefore an admission delay. The reviewed
work recognises the time this takes, but does not isolate the admission
step.

Kurma, a load balancer for geo-distributed storage systems, is
explicitly designed to operate at a granularity of seconds so that it
can \enquote{work in tandem with modern elastic controllers, thereby
reducing over-provisioning and SLO violations incurred during
provisioning delays} \parencite[p.~386]{Bogdanov2018Kurma}. The delay
between provisioning and usable capacity is therefore a recognised 
cost, yet the time a backend spends between becoming ready and 
starting to receive traffic is not measured on its own in that 
evaluation.

Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. couple auto-scaling with load balancing
in an SDN redirector for a transparent VNF cluster, so that
load balancing operates over a pool of instances that is resized while
traffic is being served. Their system changes the number of active
cluster members and removes flow rules for unhealthy instances using
status obtained through the MANO monitoring path
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.
It therefore demonstrates that dynamic membership is already a legitimate
concern. Its evaluation, however, reports activation for the whole
system, so the time between a backend becoming ready and starting to
receive traffic is not isolated in their evaluation.

Managing the pool itself is an established problem. Dreibholz and Mazumdar
apply the IETF Reliable Server Pooling framework to a pool spanning edge and
multi-cloud resources. A member registers on join and de-registers on leave,
while a registrar keeps its availability current through periodic keep-alive
checks. Their analysis notes that \enquote{propagating load updates takes time,
leading to temporary inaccuracy}, and leaves the mitigation to policies that
discount stale pool state
\parencite[p.~6]{Dreibholz2023TowardsLightweightTaskSchedulingFramework}.

Neghabi et al. record failure management as a concern that the
mechanisms their survey covers have not taken into account, so
failure-driven changes to the set of backends receiving traffic remain
unaddressed \parencite{Neghabi2018LoadBalancingMechanismsSoftwareDefined}.

The set of backends receiving traffic and the true serving state of its backends
can therefore fall out of step in two directions. When a change reaches the
routing plane late or not at all, ready capacity stays outside the set, and the
nodes already serving carry the demand without it. When a backend is in the set
while it cannot serve, the requests routed to it fail.

This dissertation treats admission into that set as the variable, because the
four admission modes require different evidence before a backend is admitted
(backend notification, notification with a fallback check, periodic polling, and
notification with a confirmation check). It measures how they affect whether and
when the new capacity starts serving requests and what users experience, under
three message conditions: the message arrives intact, never arrives, or arrives
before the backend can serve. Chapter~\ref{ch:results} evaluates the four modes
under one regime.

\section{SDN-Based Orchestration Platforms}\label{sec:lit_sdn_orchestration} %DONE
Resource orchestration is the management layer that keeps a running service
within its requirements by coordinating the compute, network, and storage
resources of the infrastructure on which it runs. Edge sites supply the
resources, SDN the programmable connectivity, containers the unit of compute, and
the document store the service state. That decision-making, and its execution, is
orchestration, and at the edge it is all the more demanding because each site
holds scarce capacity and the demand on it fluctuates. Where the two tiers are
separate, the scaling action can address either side, because compute replicas
serve requests while the data they serve lives in the storage tier, so demand for
data can exist without demand for compute and vice versa.

This dissertation studies the SDN-based form of this layer, in which the
coordination spans the SDN controller, the container runtime, and the data store,
and the platforms reviewed here bind these layers with different degrees of
coupling. This section examines how closely the three functions of the chain,
monitoring, scaling, and routing, are held together in each, using the terms
interface and coordination gap as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}.

Baktir et al. organise the gains SDN brings to edge computing into 
benefit areas such as high-resolution control built on a centralised 
view and programmatic access \parencite{Baktir2017HowCanEdgeComputingBenefit}. 
Wang et al. survey architectures in which SDN and NFV jointly coordinate placement,
traffic, and caching across the network and service layers, showing
that SDN's role at the edge extends beyond forwarding into
orchestration \parencite{Wang2019SoftwareDefinedNetworkingEnhancedEdge}.
These works document architectures or proofs of concept rather than the
coordination inside a running system, and the interfaces between the functions
fall outside their scope.


Peng et al. exercise SDN-based resource management in vehicular
multi-access edge computing as task migration and radio slicing,
treating telemetry as available input rather than as an interface to
be studied, and without container orchestration or tier scaling
\parencite{Peng2019SDNResourceManagementAutonomousVehicular}.

Some of these platforms bring the functions together at the SDN control
point itself. Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. combine monitoring, auto-scaling,
and load balancing in a single SDN redirector for a transparent VNF cluster,
although metric collection and the lifecycle operations that feed and
execute the decision remain external \parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing};
Hung et al. present an SDN controller for 5G backhaul that closes a
telemetry-and-routing loop, with telemetry collection delegated to
decentralised microservices reached through an API, omitting scaling
altogether \parencite{Hung2022SDNControllerMonitoringArchitecture5G}. 
Combining the functions at an SDN control point therefore has precedent and is
leveraged in this dissertation, but neither system reports the delay within its
coordination.

Three further systems coordinate the same functions across multiple sites.
OneEdge keeps authoritative state at each edge site, reconciles it with a central
aggregate through two-phase commits, and uses \ac{ZMQ} among its components; its
evaluation reports end-to-end deployment latency with a coarse breakdown into
scheduling delay, WAN overhead, and container creation, but not the cost of each
control-plane interaction \parencite{Saurez2021OneEdge}. McK8s runs four
controllers as Kubernetes operators over a Cilium mesh and measures
inter-cluster latencies, treating them as input to placement rather than as an
effect to study
\parencite{Tamiru2021Mck8sOrchestrationPlatformGeoDistributed}. Kurma exchanges
load, latency, and SLO-violation measurements among datacenters and redirects
requests at second granularity, naming detection, acquisition, and warm-up
before treating them as one combined quantity
\parencite{Bogdanov2018Kurma}. The coordination is built into each system, 
but the delay it carries is not isolated. None of their evaluations attributes
the time taken by the state updates, exchanges, and redirects along those
communication paths to the individual handoffs between observing demand,
deciding, provisioning, and admitting capacity.

Bringing the three functions together is therefore not itself a new concept,
but none of these systems holds the whole control process in a single
executable and exposes the handoffs between observing, deciding, provisioning,
and admitting as configurable interfaces. SDN-based resource orchestration is
nevertheless an established and feasible arrangement, and Nain et al. record
resource optimisation in SDN-based edge environments among the field's
continuing challenges \parencite{Nain2024ResourceOptimizationEdgeSDNEdge}. The
platform built in this dissertation follows that setup and exposes the
telemetry mode, scaling decisions, and readiness admission as independently
configurable interfaces, as presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:system_architecture}.

\section{Synthesis and Research Gaps}\label{sec:lit_synthesis} % DONE
Across the reviewed literature, the cost of these crossings is
acknowledged, but never measured on its own. Where a cost is measured, it is
measured for a whole system: Kurma reports provisioning delay as one combined
quantity \parencite{Bogdanov2018Kurma}, OneEdge breaks end-to-end deployment
latency into stages without attributing it to a single handoff
\parencite{Saurez2021OneEdge}, and Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. report only
whole-system activation
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}. AdapPF comes

\parencite{Huang2023AdapPFSelfAdaptiveScrapeInterval}, while the placement
literature names the interval between arrival and readiness as launch time and

\parencite{TabatabaeeMalazi2022DynamicServicePlacementMultiAccess}.

\chapter{Architecture and Design}\label{ch:system_architecture}
This chapter presents the architecture and design of the proposed
orchestration platform for edge services.
The design provides a platform that runs controller-side telemetry
consumption, elasticity coordination, and traffic steering in one controller
process per domain. The telemetry mode, the scaling decision,
and the transitions from scaling action to traffic admission
remain explicit observation points. 

Section~\ref{sec:design_requirements} defines the requirements that guided
the proposed solution, and Section~\ref{sec:architecture_overview} presents
the layered organisation of the platform and its two-domain deployment
(Figure~\ref{fig:platform_layers}).
Section~\ref{sec:elastic_allocation} describes the distributed elastic
allocation of compute and storage resources, Section~\ref{sec:monitoring_decision}
the monitoring and decision engine, and Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow}
the control workflow. Together, these sections realise the design
requirements: the domain organisation and the data paths (DR1, DR4, DR5),
the elastic allocation and the capacity changes (DR3, DR7), the

instrumented chain of the control workflow (DR2, DR8, DR9).

\section{Design Requirements}\label{sec:design_requirements}
The platform was designed to support edge services whose demand and
resource requirements vary over time. The requirements below follow from the
coordination problem and the research gaps established in
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}, and guide the design
presented in the remainder of the chapter.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textit{DR\arabic*:}, leftmargin=*]
    \item \textit{Domain-scoped control.} Each network domain must be operated
    by its own controller, which observes that domain's demand and acts on
    that domain's resources. Peer controllers exchange resource state, but the
    operation of a domain must not depend on a central entity.

    \item \textit{Single-process control loop.} Telemetry consumption, the
    scaling decision, load balancing, and the capacity changes must run in
    the same controller process, sharing their state directly rather than
    through inter-service handoffs.

    \item \textit{Dual-tier, demand-driven elasticity.} Compute and storage
    capacity must each follow their own demand and scale independently, and
    storage capacity must be changeable at run time from the platform's own
    code while the service keeps serving.

    \item \textit{Stable addresses in front of the tiers' service interfaces.}
    Clients and servers must address stable virtual addresses, one for the
    service and one for storage reads, and the controller must enforce each
    routing decision on the network path, so that capacity changes never
    change the point of access. Each address must front what its tier serves
    rather than a node: a service request must be a call to the API served by
    the compute replicas, answerable by any admitted replica, and a storage
    read a request through the interface the storage tier exposes, answerable
    by any admitted member.

    \item \textit{Two-level load distribution.} Request load must be
    distributed across the compute replicas and storage reads across the
    storage members, using the candidates' reported state, with only admitted
    capacity eligible.

    \item \textit{Hierarchical reporting with freshness bounds.} Edge servers
    and storage members must report to their domain's telemetry aggregator,
    and the controller must consume the summaries formed from these reports.
    Each summary must carry when each node last reported, and a node silent
    beyond a bound must be treated as absent, so that the controller acts
    only on capacity it can observe.

    \item \textit{Staged capacity changes.} Capacity must enter and
    leave service through staged transitions. Added capacity must be
    provisioned and registered before it can receive traffic, and removed
    capacity must be withdrawn from routing and drained of in-flight requests
    before it is released. The routing pools must contain only capacity that
    can serve.

    \item \textit{Non-blocking steering.} Packet handling must never wait on
    slow infrastructure work such as provisioning or removal.

    \item \textit{Instrumented transitions.} Every capacity change must be a
    sequence of explicit, timestamped transitions (provisioned, ready,
    admitted, serving), and every served request must be attributed to the
    node that served it, so that the chain from observed demand to the first
    request served by new capacity can be reconstructed.

    \item \textit{Configurable interfaces.} The telemetry mode, the scaling
    action, and the admission mode must be independently selectable
    configurations of the platform.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Architecture}\label{sec:architecture_overview}
Within each domain the platform is organised in three layers
(Figure~\ref{fig:platform_layers}). The control layer holds the controller,
which consumes the telemetry summaries, performs load balancing, and decides
the scaling actions. The data plane layer holds the software switch, which
carries the client requests and the storage traffic along the path the
controller decides. The resource layer holds the edge servers that
serve requests, the storage members that hold the service state, and the
telemetry aggregator that forms the summaries for the controller.

\subsection{Two-Domain Organisation}

The platform is organised as two network domains joined by an inter-domain
data path, representing the two sites of the deployment. Each domain
contains client workloads, a software data plane, a pool of edge servers, a
replicated storage tier, a telemetry aggregator, and a controller. The peer
controllers exchange topology information, and each consumes the telemetry
summaries produced by the aggregators of both domains. Each controller runs
telemetry consumption, elasticity coordination, and traffic steering in the
same process for its own domain. Where this thesis says node without
qualification, it means one deployed unit of either tier: an edge server of
the compute pool, or a storage member of the replica set.

Both domains run on one host in the evaluated deployment, so the control
plane is local and only the inter-domain data path crosses domains. The
evaluated demand stays within a domain, so the two-domain organisation is
exercised as a cross-domain-aware control plane with a single-site data path.

\subsection{Virtual IP Data Paths}\label{sec:vip_data_paths}
Clients access edge services through a per-domain \ac{VIP}. Service requests
are addressed to the virtual service address \texttt{VIP\_SERVER}, and edge
servers route storage reads through the virtual data address \texttt{VIP\_DATA}.
This separation represents service routing and data access as distinct
routing surfaces.

Neither address names a node. A request to \texttt{VIP\_SERVER} is a call
to the service API, answerable by any admitted compute replica, and a read
to \texttt{VIP\_DATA} is issued through the storage tier's data interface and
answered by whichever member the controller selects; the client and the edge
server address the tier, and the controller decides the node.

The controller is responsible for both routing surfaces meaning, for each new
connection it selects the unit that will serve it among the admitted units of
the relevant tier, and it places that choice on the network path. Established
connections keep their unit, so a capacity change does not reroute traffic
that is already being served. Load balancing may select a backend in either
domain (a platform capability held constant in the evaluation), while
cross-domain storage placement is not exercised.

Load balancing is cost-based, for both service backends and storage
members. The controller compares the candidates' reported resource state
under configurable weights and selects the lowest-cost unit, preferring local
candidates at comparable state. For storage reads observed on the virtual
data path, the same mechanism selects a replica-set member. The metrics of
the comparison, and how the selection is enforced on the path, are described
in Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}.

A request's latency decomposes into the \emph{pre-dispatch wait}, from the
client issuing the request to its dispatch in the serving process, and the
\emph{edge service time}, from dispatch to response finalisation, which
decomposes into a \emph{data-access} and an \emph{edge-processing}
component. Their sum is the request's \emph{user-visible latency}, the
client-side round trip. The two edge-service components are the
tier-specific latency signals the scaling policy observes
(Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}).

\subsection{Control and Telemetry Paths}\label{sec:control_paths}
Edge servers and storage components emit resource and request observations to
their local telemetry aggregator, and each aggregator forms
summaries for the controller. These summaries inform both load
balancing and elasticity decisions, while topology exchange
maintains awareness of peer-domain resources.
The aggregation-to-controller link is the configurable telemetry
interface, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:telemetry_delivery_paths}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/src/telemetry_delivery_paths.drawio.png}
    \caption{Control and telemetry paths: edge servers and storage members
    send observations to the per-domain telemetry aggregator, whose

    The architecture therefore separates the request data path, the telemetry

\section{Distributed Elastic Allocation of Resources}\label{sec:elastic_allocation}
The platform scales capacity along two dimensions: compute replicas (also
referred to as backends in the routing context), which serve requests, and
storage members, which hold the service state. Each dimension
has its own observation signals, its own action, and its own admission and
release procedure, so that pressure on the two tiers is addressed separately.
The two dimensions are summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:elastic_allocation} and
described in turn.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/src/elastic_allocation.drawio.png}
    \caption{Elastic allocation of resources: each tier is scaled on its own
    observation signals, with compute replicas provisioned and drained and

\subsection{Scaling Policy}\label{sec:scaling_policy}
The policy is applied per resource tier, with tier-specific
signals and parameters. Each tier is observed through two signals: a
utilisation signal, which in this platform is the CPU utilisation of the
tier's units, and a latency signal. For each summary $w$, let
$c(w)$ denote the summary's utilisation and $t(w)$ its typical latency
(the tier's latency statistic over the summary, configurable as the mean or
the median). Each signal is first mapped onto the unit
interval through two calibration parameters, a floor and a span. A value at
or below its floor contributes nothing, a value at or above the floor plus
its span contributes the maximum, and intermediate values contribute
proportionally. The floor separates normal operation from pressure, and the
span sets how far the signal  must rise before its contribution saturates.
Because both signals are mapped onto this bounded scale, they contribute on
common terms despite different units, and a single extreme
summary cannot dominate the score. The two normalised
components are combined with weights $w_{\mathrm{util}}$ and
$w_{\mathrm{lat}}$ into the degradation score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degradation_score}
\begin{split}
    s(w) &= w_{\mathrm{util}}\,\hat{c}(w) + w_{\mathrm{lat}}\,\hat{t}(w), \\
    \hat{c}(w) &= \min\left(1,\ \max\left(0,\ \frac{c(w)-c_0}{c_\Delta}\right)\right), \\
    \hat{t}(w) &= \min\left(1,\ \max\left(0,\ \frac{t(w)-t_0}{t_\Delta}\right)\right),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with the per-tier weights configured to sum to one, so the score lies
between zero and one. A signal at or below its floor contributes nothing and
is treated as normal for the tier; $c_0$ and $t_0$ are those floors, and
$c_\Delta$ and $t_\Delta$ the rises above them at which each contribution
saturates. The normalised components $\hat{c}(w)$ and $\hat{t}(w)$ therefore
lie in $[0,1]$.
    The floors, spans, weights, and threshold parameters are configured
independently for each tier, so the policy can be calibrated to each tier
without changing the structure of the score. For the compute tier the signals are CPU
utilisation and request-processing latency, while for the storage tier
they are storage-member CPU utilisation and data-access latency. These
signals feed only the scaling decisions, while load balancing
uses the reported resource state described in
Section~\ref{sec:vip_data_paths}.

    A summary counts as degraded when its score reaches an adaptive
threshold, and a scale-up requires sustained degradation across recent summaries. 
Let $\mathcal{W}_n$ be the set of the last $W$ summaries in which the tier was 
eligible to act, and $R$ the required number of degraded summaries; the scale-up 
condition is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scale_up_condition}
\begin{split}
    \left|\,\{\, w \in \mathcal{W}_n : s(w) \geq \theta(k) \,\}\right| &\geq R, \\
    \theta(k) &= \min\big(\theta_{\mathrm{base}} + k\,\Delta\theta + \beta_{\mathrm{peer}},\ \theta_{\max}\big),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the number of dynamic units currently in the tier's pool,
$\theta_{\mathrm{base}}$ the base threshold, $\Delta\theta$ the increment per
additional unit, $\theta_{\max}$ its upper bound, and $\beta_{\mathrm{peer}}$
the peer relief added to the compute-tier threshold while the peer domain is
healthy enough to absorb spillover traffic (zero otherwise). The increment is
applied linearly for the compute tier; for the storage tier it diminishes with
each additional unit, down to a configured minimum.
The threshold follows the number of dynamic units in the tier's pool.
Each additional unit therefore faces a higher bar, and the threshold falls
back as units are removed. A scale-up cooldown after each committed scale-up
and a scale-down cooldown keyed on the most recent scale-up keep the pace of
actions bounded, together with a per-tier cap on the number of units. The
set advances only when the tier was eligible to act, and it is cleared when
a scale-up occurs. A committed scale-up also
clears the tier's scale-down window, so a recent scale-up is not immediately
reversed.

    A summary counts as idle when both signals fall below their idle
thresholds,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:idle_condition}
    c(w) < \tau_c \quad\text{and}\quad t(w) < \tau_t,
\end{equation}
where $\tau_c$ and $\tau_t$ are the idle thresholds of the utilisation and
latency signals.
A scale-down is requested only when idleness is sustained across
$R_\downarrow$ of the last $W_\downarrow$ summaries, where $R_\downarrow$ is the
required number of idle summaries and $W_\downarrow$ the length of the
scale-down window. This window is longer than the scale-up one, which
prevents a transient data-bound stall from being misread as idle capacity. 
Summaries whose latency exceeds a ceiling are
treated as indeterminate and do not count toward idleness.

The policy evaluates each tier per summary, in a fixed order:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
    \item the summary is skipped when the tier is ineligible: an
    infrastructure operation is in progress, the tier is at its cap, or a
    scale-up cooldown is active;
    \item the degradation score $s(w)$ is computed from the two normalised
    signals~\eqref{eq:degradation_score};
    \item if at least $R$ of the last $W$ eligible summaries reach the adaptive
    threshold $\theta(k)$~\eqref{eq:scale_up_condition}, the tier requests
    a scale-up and the summary history is cleared;
    \item otherwise, if both signals fall below their idle
    thresholds~\eqref{eq:idle_condition}, idleness is tracked, and the
    tier requests a scale-down once idleness spans $R_\downarrow$ of the
    last $W_\downarrow$ summaries.
\end{enumerate}
    The values these parameters take in the evaluation are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}.

\subsection{Compute}\label{sec:elastic_compute}
Compute capacity is a per-domain pool of edge servers, each
capable of serving any request addressed to the domain's virtual service
address. Storage reads performed by a compute replica are routed through the
domain's virtual data address, or served from a Tier~1 copy where one is
held (the data-placement tiers are defined in Section~\ref{sec:elastic_data}).
Reads are left to the routing path. Compute scale-up
applies the policy defined in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}.

A newly provisioned compute replica is recorded through an explicit
registration step and becomes eligible for load balancing only once it is
visible in the routing pool. How readiness reaches that pool is the
admission interface, and it has four modes: backend notification admits on
the message the backend sends, notification with a fallback check adds a
check after a configured wait, periodic polling ignores the message and
checks on a fixed interval, and notification with a confirmation check lets
the message wake a check that must pass. The modes admit on evidence of
different strength, so a message that arrives before the replica can serve
reaches the routing pool unchecked under the modes that trust it and is
observed by the modes that check. They are measured under three message
conditions, each held for the whole run: the message arrives intact, never
arrives, or arrives before the backend can serve. Because load balancing
steers new connections while established ones remain with their unit
(Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow}), admission also marks the point at which
the new backend can begin to share the load.

Compute scale-down removes the least disruptive eligible replica, applying
the idle condition defined in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}. 
Only replicas provisioned by the platform are eligible for removal, so the
original nodes are left undisturbed. A candidate is considered
only while its reports are recent, and among the eligible replicas the one
carrying the least request load is removed first.
Removal proceeds in two phases: the replica is first withdrawn
from the routing pool and signalled to finish its in-flight requests,
and only after it drains is it released.
A pending drain can be cancelled if load rebounds before the replica exits,
favouring fast recovery over aggressive reclamation.

\subsection{Storage}\label{sec:elastic_data}
The storage tier of each domain is a replica set, and storage capacity is
scaled by changing its membership, one member at a time. Any admitted member
can answer a read, so read load is distributed across the membership. Writes
are not distributed: the store keeps a single primary for writes, and the
remaining members replicate the data and serve eventually consistent reads.
This write placement follows from the store's replication model rather than
from a platform design decision. The evaluation adopts it as the tier's
consistency contract and does not rely on read-after-write semantics. Storage
scale-up is a membership change. The controller provisions a new storage
member, which joins the set, and admits it to the data path only once the
member can serve reads. Preparation is decoupled from the reaction as a readied
member is held in reserve outside the routing pool, and the storage scale-up is
the activation of this prepared reserve, which removes the initial readiness wait
from the reaction path. A new reserve is prepared immediately afterwards.

Admission alone does not put the member to work, because a connection
chooses its member when it is opened, so the platform renews the edge
servers' storage connections on admission and spreads new connections across
members whose reported cost is close to the best.

Storage scale-down removes an idle member while the remaining members keep
serving. Only members added by the platform are eligible for removal, so the
set's original membership is left undisturbed, and the most recently added
eligible member is removed first. The decision applies the idle condition
defined in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy} to the storage tier's idle signals
(its data-access latency and, in the configurations that enable
it, its CPU utilisation).

Beyond the membership change, the platform defines three data-placement
tiers: Tier~0 provides direct remote reads over the virtual data path, 
meaning the replica-set member is deployed outside its
primary network domain, Tier~1 holds a partial copy of the data in the consumer
domain, and Tier~2 is the replica-set extension described above. Tier~0 and
Tier~1 are platform capabilities rather than evaluated treatments. The
evaluated storage action is limited to activating the prepared
reserve or removing a member of the domain's own replica set.

\section{Telemetry, Load Balancing, and Scaling}\label{sec:monitoring_decision}
The producers and the aggregator that collects their reports are described
in Section~\ref{sec:control_paths}. Each edge server reports after every
request it answers, carrying its own resource state and the experience of
that request, and each storage member reports as it serves storage requests,
so a busy node reports often and a quiet one is kept visible by
its periodic heartbeat. The aggregator produces a summary of the most recent
period at a fixed interval. Every summary, including those covering idle
periods, is evaluated and recorded. Each summary
also carries the time of each node's last report,
so a node that stays silent beyond an absence bound is treated as gone
instead of being acted upon.

The controller consumes these summaries through an explicit, configurable
interface. Its four modes are the two defects of Section~\ref{sec:lit_monitoring}
separately and together, implemented as four configurations of one interface
rather than as two transports opposed to each other, and they supply the same
summaries in different forms.

Because the decision engine evaluates delivered summaries only, the mode sets
the earliest moment a scaling reaction can begin, and because the condition
counts the last summaries the engine has seen, missing summaries slow how
often it is met as well as when it first can be. The aggregation, the
workload, and the decision logic remain unchanged across modes, so any
difference in how the controller scales is attributed to the evidence it
received rather than to the mechanism that carried it.

Inside the controller, the summaries feed two consumers. Load balancing keeps
its candidate state from them, so a unit is selected on its current load
rather than on the state it had when it was admitted. The decision engine
applies the scaling policy of Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy} to each
delivered summary, and reads both domains. The tier condition is assessed on
the local domain's summary, while the peer domain's condition adjusts the
threshold through the peer-relief term of
Equation~\eqref{eq:scale_up_condition}, so a scale-up is decided on the state
of the system rather than on the local site alone. Once a tier's condition is
sustained, the engine emits an action request.
Figure~\ref{fig:decision_engine_flow} details the
per-summary evaluation, from the eligibility check to the request emission.

The decision engine may also be configured to commit to a single scaling 
action (compute or storage), which is the scaling interface of the demand-to-capacity chain. 
In the bottleneck-aware configuration, that choice distinguishes between compute-bound and
data-access-bound demand and scales the matching tier when it leads by the
configured classification margin, resolving close scores towards storage. 
A per-tier action budget can bound its actions,
and the action taken and the actions rejected are recorded as evidence. The
platform's capacity events, such as the completion of a drain or the
readiness of a storage member, reach the same decision path, so that
admission and release follow the same flow as the actions themselves.

\section{Control Workflow}\label{sec:control_workflow}
The control workflow separates three execution contexts that share one
process but do not block one another. The packet context handles routing
decisions and does not wait on slow work. The observation context evaluates
delivered summaries and emits action requests. The action context performs
infrastructure changes such as provisioning, drain, and removal.

The workflow traverses the demand-to-capacity chain introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}. A change in demand alters the request
experience reported by the compute replicas, and the summary that contains
the change is produced and delivered. The decision engine evaluates the
delivered summary. If the tier condition is sustained, the engine emits a
scaling action request, which the action context executes. The provisioned unit
reaches its readiness state and is admitted to the corresponding routing
pool, which for the storage tier is the activation of the prepared reserve.
New connections can then be directed to it, while established connections
remain where they were placed. The chain completes when the new unit
serves its first successful request. Each step is instrumented, so the
timeline from demand observation to usable capacity is recorded end to end,
from registration and routing-pool inclusion to the first successful request
(Figure~\ref{fig:control_workflow}). When readiness admission is enabled, a
compute replica that does not reach readiness within the configured bound, or
that is never admitted within the abandonment bound, is abandoned and torn
down instead of being admitted. Without readiness admission,
compute replicas are registered as soon as they are provisioned and no
readiness bound applies, so a replica that never becomes usable is reclaimed
only by the ordinary capacity checks.

    \caption{The control workflow, drawn as a UML diagram. The
    demand-to-capacity chain runs across three contexts in the same process:
    the packet context, the observation context, and the action context. The

    \label{fig:control_workflow}

State crosses these contexts through shared structures rather than direct
calls, and the packet context reads them on each routing decision. Because it
never waits on the action context, a long-running change cannot stall request
steering, and because the action requests are queued and prioritised,
detection is not blocked by execution.

Every request that adds or preserves capacity
precedes every request that removes capacity. The cancellation of a
pending drain precedes its execution. 
This execution order is distinct from
the scaling decision, which the observation context decides before the
request is queued.

Across domains, the peer controllers exchange topology state so that each
controller is aware of the peer domain's resources, while acting on its own
domain's demand. The concrete realisation of this design, the technologies
that implement each context, and the validation of the resulting platform
are the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. The evaluation of the
three interfaces is presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

The two domains are joined by a router container whose inter-domain link

available and off by default). The emulated link carries the cross-domain
data path, and every control-plane link is host-local.

The load is exerted by a scheduled driver that runs on the host beside
the testbed. Each client is a Linux network namespace attached to its
domain's client network, and the driver issues HTTP calls from it against the
domain's virtual service address, so the controller observes the source
address and steers each request through the data path. A run instantiates 24
clients per domain, 48 in total, and executes the run's phase schedule
(Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}). Active clients issue requests
continuously at the phase rate, against users and content items drawn from the
seeded snapshot, so traffic generation does not depend on a live database and
the run seed fixes the active clients and their request streams.

The delivery of the load is open-loop, so a client keeps issuing its requests
on schedule while earlier requests are still outstanding and the offered
demand is preserved when the service slows down. Three client-side bounds make
the failure modes explicit and finite: each client keeps at most 1024 requests
in flight, the full window records a new request as dropped, every request
carries a 300~s client-side cap and is recorded as a timeout at that cap, and
in-flight requests that outlast the 30~s phase-end drain are abandoned and
recorded as cancelled. Each issued request is written to the run's request
record with its phase, kind, targets, issue time, outcome class and latency,
which is the raw material of the per-run analyses of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Data Path}\label{sec:impl_data_path}

The data plane runs OpenFlow on Open vSwitch. It must match ordinary
addresses and ports,
rewrite each connection to its backend, and deliver new connections to
the controller, which answers from the same Python process that runs the
control logic (DR2). OpenFlow covers this set natively, and it reaches
the host's connection tracking, so per-connection identity and address
translation live in the kernel rather than in the rules. A programmable data
plane such as P4 could express the same steering, but its additional
capabilities serve network-level questions that this thesis does not ask,
because every decision it evaluates is taken in the controller.

Each domain has two virtual service addresses, one for client requests
and one for storage reads. The data plane combines OpenFlow rules with
the kernel's connection tracking. On the service path, the controller
installs a DNAT/SNAT rule pair for the client and the selected backend.
With flow isolation enabled the pair is scoped to a single connection and
removed after its response, so every measured request triggers a fresh
load-balancing decision. On the data path, a forward rule selects the member and
commits a NAT mapping to it, and the kernel records the connection by its
five-tuple. Reply packets are handed back to connection tracking, which
reverses the mapping, so the identity of each connection lives in the
kernel's table rather than in the rule. Two consequences matter for the platform.

Established connections survive rule deletion, which lets the controller
bulk-delete a member's forward rules when the member leaves the pool and
lets new connections re-select while established ones finish. And because
the kernel keeps connections apart by their full five-tuple, the rule can
be scoped to the client or, as in the reported configuration, to a single
connection of it, so a pooled client spreads its connections across
members. Each domain tracks its connections in its own zone, and rules
that belong to a domain share a cookie so they can be removed together.

\section{Distributed Elastic Allocation of Resources}\label{sec:impl_elastic}

\subsection{Compute}\label{sec:impl_compute}

A compute replica is a Flask application served by the Werkzeug server.
It exposes the workload routes, which are the service API that
\texttt{VIP\_SERVER} fronts, a drain endpoint that moves the replica into a
draining state, and a readiness endpoint. The readiness predicate is a real
service round-trip. The endpoint answers 200 only while a MongoDB ping against
the domain's virtual data address succeeds, and the server binds before
readiness is declared, so the predicate means servability and the readiness
message cannot precede the socket. After each request the replica pushes one
telemetry record with the request's outcome and timing (total time, and the
MongoDB time split into reads and writes) and its own CPU and memory use, read
from the container's cgroup files. With readiness messages enabled it also
emits an \texttt{app\_ready} control event when the predicate first succeeds.

The controller provisions a replica by starting its container with the tier's
quotas and the run's environment, attaching it to the domain network through an
Open vSwitch port, and recording it as a backend. Removal follows the two phases
of Section~\ref{sec:elastic_allocation}. The replica carrying the least request
load is removed first, with the lowest utilisation and request-processing latency
as tie-breakers, and its network attachment is removed only after its in-flight
requests reach zero and a quiet period passes.

Readiness admission is implemented by a component that holds newly spawned
replicas in a pending registry and admits them to the routing pool only when
its configured rule is satisfied. The rule is selected by a propagation mode,
running the four admission modes defined in
Section~\ref{sec:elastic_compute}, and the configuration literals behind each
mode, with the fallback wait, the polling interval and the confirmation check,
are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}. The admission decision is
summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:readiness_admission}.

Checks retry at a fixed interval with their own timeout. A replica that
is not admitted within the abandonment bound is abandoned and fully torn
down. Every admission records its source in the admission log, the
message, a poll, a fallback check, or a confirmation check. A check that
disagrees with an admission made on the message is recorded as a warning
and the admission stands, because the message already admitted the
replica. The confirmation check never admits unverified. The admission
decision is summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:readiness_admission}.

\subsection{Storage}\label{sec:impl_data}

The storage tier is built on MongoDB replica sets, because DR3 requires storage
capacity to change at run time from the platform's own code while the service
keeps serving. MongoDB answers that change as a replica-set reconfiguration
command on the connection the platform already holds, with one container per
member. The alternatives place the operation elsewhere. MySQL InnoDB Cluster has
it, but only in the AdminAPI of MySQL Shell, a separate management client the
platform would have to invoke
\parencite{MySQLDocsAdminAPI,MySQLDocsInnoDBCluster}. A PostgreSQL
streaming-replication standby has no membership operation at all, so adding or
removing a replica must be built by hand
\parencite{PostgreSQLDocsWarmStandby}.

Each domain's storage is a MongoDB replica set of its own, built in three
steps. At network build time, the domain's storage container starts as
the set's single member and becomes its primary, and the run's data set
is seeded through it. At runtime, a new member starts as a bare
\texttt{mongod} with a companion sidecar. The sidecar connects to a known
member of the set, appends itself as a non-voting member with zero
priority so it cannot affect elections, and applies the change with a
single \texttt{replSetReconfig} call. When the member reaches the
secondary state and can serve reads, the sidecar emits the
\texttt{rs\_secondary\_ready} control event, and the controller admits
the member into the domain's virtual data address, by default on the
event, with a telemetry-observed secondary state as the fallback path.
While the member serves, the sidecar reports a sample whenever the member's
operation counters show activity, carrying replication lag, open connections,
and the container's CPU and memory use, and an idle member is kept visible by
the periodic heartbeat. Removal reverses the steps. The member leaves the
routing pool first, and the controller then performs \texttt{rs.remove()}
against the primary, waits for the removal to be confirmed, and tears the
container down.
The interface the virtual data address fronts is the database's own API:
the edge server issues its reads through the MongoDB driver against the
address, any admitted member can answer them, and the driver never learns
the physical membership.

When the persistent reserve is enabled, the activated member is already
joined and servable, and its replacement is prepared once the slot is
free. The member lifecycle is bounded by the run's caps
(Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}).

Reads are issued against the domain's virtual data address through
per-request leases that the edge server manages per domain epoch. On every
storage admission the controller sends a renewal request to each edge
server of the domain, and the edge rotates the epoch so that new
connections choose a member again. The request is sent in two waves, one
immediately and one 45~s later, after the 30~s warm lease, because a
connection opened just before the admission keeps its member for the life
of that lease. Selection of a member uses a band: costs within 3~\% of the
lowest count as equal, the band keeps at least two members, and the choice
inside the band rotates. Reads prefer a secondary.
Writes are issued directly to the replica-set primary. Selective
synchronisation (Tier~1), which holds a
partial copy of the data in the consumer domain, and cross-domain
storage placement, which places a replica-set member in the peer
domain, are capabilities of Section~\ref{sec:elastic_data} and are
disabled in the reported experiments.

\section{Telemetry, Load Balancing, and Scaling}\label{sec:impl_monitoring}

Each edge server reports after every request it answers, and each storage
member reports as it serves storage operations, so the telemetry path is
not flooded with idle traffic. The domain's aggregator collects these reports
and produces a summary of the preceding 10~s every 10~s. A heartbeat every
60~s keeps a quiet but alive node visible, and a starting node is visible
before its first request.

    The configurable interface of Section~\ref{sec:monitoring_decision} is
implemented as alternative sources over the same summaries, so the four
modes differ only in how the same summaries reach the controller. Every
summary is appended to a durable, sequence-numbered log that the replaying
modes read. Table~\ref{tab:impl_delivery_sources} lists the four modes and
their implemented sources.

The platform's default source pushes each summary as it is produced, without a
delivery guarantee, while the campaign's push mode uses the acknowledged replay,
so the campaign can verify that every produced summary arrived. The replaying
sources record every summary in a delivery log with its network, sequence number,
close time, delivery time, delay, and source mode, which is what the telemetry
experiments read. Two kinds of message stay outside the
telemetry modes. Control events are the platform's own notifications,
forwarded by the aggregator immediately: a replica reporting that it can
serve (\texttt{app\_ready}) or that its in-flight requests have finished
(\texttt{drain\_complete}), a storage member reporting that it is ready to
serve reads (\texttt{rs\_secondary\_ready}), and, where per-request flow
isolation is enabled, each completed request (\texttt{request\_complete}),
which releases its flow so the next request is routed afresh.

A collector writes per-run resource records for each domain, debug records and
per-node files, and samples the CPU and memory of both controller instances. The
controller's own records complete the timing evidence: the lifecycle of each
provisioned unit, the elasticity events, the admission log, and the decision log,
kept per run beside the request record of
Section~\ref{sec:impl_infrastructure} and the delivery log above. Inside the controller, the delivered
summaries feed the two consumers of
Section~\ref{sec:monitoring_decision}, and
every decision is written to the decision log for the summary that produced
it, together with its evidence. In the
bottleneck-aware configuration, the action gate commits at most one
action per summary and records the bottleneck class it assigned, the
per-tier evidence, the action taken and the actions rejected, and the budget
counters. Absence
detection and scale-down evaluation run on a fixed ticker once per
delivered summary rather than when it arrives. The configuration surface
is environment-driven, and its values are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}.

\section{Control Workflow}\label{sec:impl_control}

Each domain runs its own controller instance. The three execution
contexts of Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow} are implemented as one
cooperative event loop and two native threads. The packet path handles
flow decisions inside the OpenFlow event loop and never blocks on
infrastructure work. The telemetry consumer runs on the same event
loop: it receives the summaries, updates the per-backend and
per-member state, evaluates the tier conditions, and posts typed alerts
to a priority queue. The elasticity thread is a daemon thread that blocks
on that queue and performs the infrastructure changes, and the readiness
gate runs its own worker thread for checks.

Each alert identifies its resource tier and its action, and the order of
Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow} is refined by tier. Among additions, a
storage scale-up precedes a compute scale-up, and among removals, a storage
scale-down precedes a compute scale-down.

State crosses the contexts through the shared structures of
Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow}. The telemetry consumer writes the
per-node state and the alerts between its yield points, the elasticity
thread hands its results to the routing pools through the same
registration the packet path reads, and the packet path reads the pools
on each flow decision.

On the first packet of a new connection, no flow rule matches and the packet is
sent to the controller, which selects the lowest-cost unit among the eligible
candidates under the cost function of Section~\ref{sec:vip_data_paths}.

For backends the cost uses CPU utilisation, memory usage, request count and
hop distance. For storage members, CPU utilisation, memory usage, connections,
replication lag and hop distance. Each metric enters as a fraction of the
largest value observed among the eligible candidates, so metrics with
different units contribute on a common scale, ties at the lowest cost are
broken in round-robin, and a peer-domain candidate is always assigned more
hops than a local one, so local candidates are preferred at comparable
resource state. The controller then installs the rules
described in Section~\ref{sec:impl_data_path} and sends the packet
on. Subsequent packets follow the installed rules, which carry a configurable
idle timeout, resetting while the connection stays active, and a hard timeout,
after which the next packet triggers a fresh load-balancing decision (values in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}). When a unit leaves a pool, its rules are
removed. Two behaviours accompany the cost
function: a warm-lease priority, which gives a newly admitted backend
bounded priority for new connections during a configured window, and a
slow-start ramp, in which a newly discovered backend is held back by a
cost penalty that decays over the same window.
Figure~\ref{fig:vip_routing_sequence} walks through the first-packet path on
both virtual addresses.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:first_packet} summarises the first-packet path.

A request waits ahead of the edge service in three places, which the
instrumentation separates. The first is the connection setup through the
virtual service address. The second is the first packet's round trip to
the controller and the installation of its rules. The third is the queue
on the selected replica before the application reads the request. The
first two are part of the steering path described here, and the third
accrues on the serving side. The waiting time is read as a difference,
user-visible latency minus the reported edge service time.

The controller keeps its state in memory, so a restart begins from an empty
state and re-derives the topology, the routing pools, and the per-node state
from the network, flushing the stale flows and reinstalling the catch-all
rule. The evaluated runs therefore exclude mid-run controller restarts.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:experimental_setup}
This section defines the conditions under which the three research questions'
experimental evaluations were executed.

\subsection{Testbed and Instrumentation}
The evaluation was performed on the platform of
Chapters~\ref{ch:system_architecture} and~\ref{ch:implementation}, deployed as
a two-domain testbed on a single host. Each domain runs its own data plane,
compute replicas, storage tier, telemetry aggregator, and \ac{SDN}
controller, and the two domains are joined by an emulated inter-domain link. Both
domains are driven by the same open-loop driver of
Section~\ref{sec:impl_infrastructure}. The deployed components
are described there, and the per-campaign
caps, quotas, and parameter values are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}.

Each run is an independent experimental unit and starts from the same nodes
and configuration. Before each run, the data set is generated from a fixed
seed and loaded into the domain's replica-set primary, 3000 content items and
100 user profiles per domain, with the collection indexes created by the same
setup. The setup exports a snapshot of the seeded items and profiles, and the
driver draws its targets from it, so the offered demand is fixed by the run
seed and does not depend on the live database. Between runs, the containers,
the data state, and the controller state are reset.

During a run, the platform records the timeline of the demand-to-capacity
chain end to end, from demand observation to the first request served by new
capacity. The records behind these timestamps are described in
Section~\ref{sec:impl_monitoring}, and every timing reported in this chapter is
read from them. The rest of this section defines the
workloads that drive these timelines, the outcomes extracted from them,
and the design under which each campaign varied exactly one interface.

\subsection{Metrics and Outcome Definitions}\label{sec:metrics_definitions}

Every outcome is read from the recorded timeline of each run, and unless
stated otherwise each run contributes the mean of its two domains.
Outcome are reported through offered requests, completed requests,
failed requests, timeouts (each recorded at the client-side cap of 300~s), and
client-side cancellations and drops at phase end. Completion is reported on two
bases, completed over non-abandoned requests and completed over offered
requests, and latency percentiles use the served basis, where a served request
is a completed request with a successful HTTP status. A latency component is a
per-window mean over the campaign's demand episode, and each run is summarised
by the median of its episode windows.

Usable capacity is the first moment at which new capacity serves traffic,
measured from the anchor each campaign's interface defines: the onset of the
demand shift where the telemetry mode varies, and the scaling decision where
admission is under test. The remaining outcomes belong to the campaign that
varies the interface they describe, and are defined with its results. Relief is
the fall in the receiving tier's utilisation for a storage action and in the
processing component for a compute action, while the added units carry served
requests. Control overhead is reported as the CPU utilisation, resident memory,
and volume of control records of the controller processes.

\subsection{Campaign Designs and Analysis}\label{sec:campaign_analysis}
All three campaigns follow the same discipline of single-variable
manipulation. Each campaign varies exactly one interface of the
demand-to-capacity chain and holds the other two constant, so that the
measured differences are attributable to the interface under test.

The treatments, primary outcomes, screening rules, and analysis tests of every
campaign were fixed in writing before data collection.

Each campaign organises its runs so that every planned contrast is
measured over matched demand. A seed fixes the demand of a run: which clients
are active in each phase and each client's request stream, targets, payloads,
and timing jitter. The runs are organised into blocks, a block holding one run
of every treatment the campaign compares, sharing one seed, so the treatments
entering a contrast meet identical demand. The execution order within a block
is counterbalanced across blocks, so testbed drift cannot align with a single
treatment.

Every campaign uses the same replication standard, seven
seed-matched blocks per treatment, one run per treatment per block, and each
block uses a distinct seed. The seed ranges are given in each campaign's design
table. A campaign with two demand episodes runs one block set per episode over
the same seven seeds. Seven is the smallest size at which a fully separated
contrast remains resolvable under the pre-registered test: under complete
separation between two treatment sets, the exact Mann--Whitney $U$ test has a
$p$-value floor of $2/\binom{2n}{n}$, 0.0006 at seven runs per treatment.

The run-level contrasts use the exact Mann--Whitney $U$ test. A $p$-value is
reported only where the runs of the two treatments overlap in range, and a
complete separation is reported as such, since further replicates can only
preserve or weaken it. The telemetry and readiness admission campaigns report
Cliff's $\delta$ alongside, because a $p$-value alone does not show how far
apart the runs are: $\delta$ does not depend on the number of runs, a $\delta$
near $0$ means the two treatments occupy the same range, and $\delta=\pm1$
means complete separation.

Screening followed the pre-registered per-run checks, and the resulting
exclusions, their effect on the analysis population, and any pre-registered
criterion that was not met are reported with the results of the campaign
concerned. No exclusion rests on the treated outcome. The full design of each
campaign is summarised at the start of its results section, the workload is in
Table~\ref{tab:campaign_workloads}, and the replication is the standard fixed
above.


\section{Telemetry Modes (RQ1)}\label{sec:results_rq1_delivery}

The campaign evaluated how the four telemetry modes change the way the
controller scales the service when load rises. The results are reported in
terms of what the controller saw, how much capacity it added and when the new
capacity served traffic, and what users experienced.

The modes were compared over the same load rise (Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}). 
Push (A) is the reference: every summary reaches the controller as it closes. 
Delayed push (B) delivers every summary, 30~s late. Sampled push (D) delivers 
one summary in three, on time. Polling (C) delivers the same one-in-three 
share as sampled push, but the summary it returns had already closed when 
the read began, so its evidence arrives old as well as sparse (Section~\ref
{sec:monitoring_decision}). Relative to the reference, one property changes at 
a time: delay without missing summaries (B), missing summaries without delay (D),
and both together (C), so the cost of late telemetry is separated from the cost of 
missing telemetry. The pre-registered expectation was the ordering A $<$ B $<$ C
on both usable capacity and the episode latency p95, with polling and sampled
push expected to be indistinguishable from each other.

\section{Bottleneck-Aware Scaling Action (RQ2)}\label{sec:results_rq2_action}

The campaign evaluated what following the press buys over always scaling the
same tier. Four scaling policies were compared under two demand episodes:
no scaling, compute-only, storage-only, and bottleneck-aware selection,
which scales compute and storage according to the pressing demand. Each
policy was run once per episode, over seven seed-matched blocks. The
episodes differ in their request mix. The mixed press carries
interactive requests with content lookups, so both tiers are loaded. The
data-access press is read-heavy content serving, so the reads land on the
storage tier.

Each policy is a way to spend capacity. No scaling is the platform without
elasticity. It shows what the press does alone and is the reference for
every other policy. Compute-only is the usual reflex, the single elastic
dimension of a plain container platform. Storage-only is the storage tier as
the elastic dimension, the activation of a prepared reserve while the
compute tier stays fixed. Bottleneck-aware selection is the platform under
test. Both tiers can be scaled, the action follows the per-tier telemetry,
and one tier is committed per action, so an episode that presses both tiers
can be answered on both in sequence. A fixed policy can never scale the
other tier.

does not bind did not reach users. The bottleneck-aware policy scaled storage

\section{Readiness Admission Modes (RQ3)}\label{sec:results_rq3_readiness}

The campaign compared the ways a newly provisioned backend is admitted
into the routing pool, that is, added to the set of backends that can
receive traffic. The backend announces that it can serve by sending a
message, and the four modes differ in how much they trust it. In
practice the message can be dropped on the way, or arrive before the
backend can serve, so the modes are compared under three message
conditions, each against its pre-registered expectation
(Table~\ref{tab:rq3_design}); the plateau is the readiness row of
Table~\ref{tab:campaign_workloads}.

The modes differ in what the message is trusted to do
(Section~\ref{sec:elastic_compute}). The 20~s wait and the 10~s period were
calibrated in
implementation testing: both sit well inside the abandonment bound, and
the wait is long enough that a message still in flight is not mistaken
for a lost one.

Notification with a fallback check behaves like backend notification
while the message arrives, because its fallback only fires when none
does, so the priced contrast is between admitting on the message and
checking for it. Backend notification admitted each backend essentially as
soon as it could serve, periodic polling only at the next check, and every
backend was admitted earlier under backend notification ($\delta = -1.000$).
The full chain is in Table~\ref{tab:rq3_delays} and
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3_admission_intervals}.

Provisioning to the first successful response, across both start-up classes,
was about six seconds shorter under backend notification (exact
$p = 0.0005$), and relief reached the loaded tier about four seconds sooner
(complete separation, $\delta = -1.000$; the set's standardised replicate
reproduced the direction, earlier for every backend it measured). Once admitted, both modes served the first
request in about a second, and the load left the existing nodes by the
same amount under both (relief $p = 0.209$): the mode changes when the
redistribution begins, not how much it moves.
Periodic polling pays its polling granularity and nothing more: with
the period widened from 10~s to 15~s (six runs), the admission interval
moved from 9.62~s to 15.22~s and the time from the scaling decision to
usable capacity from 11.45~s to 16.97~s, both by about the added five
seconds (descriptive, Cliff's $\delta = -0.444$). The delay stayed
inside the spare capacity of the existing nodes, the margin that the
lost-message part of the campaign deliberately removes.

No user-visible cost of the seconds-level delay appeared in any tested 
healthy regime. Its backends reached the first successful response in 
a median of 4.0~s against  4.1~s under backend notification, a near-tie, 
and close to six seconds before periodic polling (10.0~s). The comparison 
covers only backends that bind in under a second:
the stall of about ten seconds at socket bind occurs in the campaign's
earlier runs and in none of its later ones, and the confirmation check's
runs, which all come from the later period, contain no stalled backend.

Read across the campaigns, the measured effects share a shape. They appear at
the edge of the operating envelope, where a margin is consumed, and not in the
steady state. The telemetry modes were indistinguishable outside the load rise,
and the cost of delay appeared only when the absorption margin of the existing
nodes ran out. The action improved user-visible quality only where it removed
the queue the episode formed. The admission delay cost nothing while the nodes
held spare capacity, and the lost message consumed that margin under the
reduced headroom the campaign fixed. On the evaluated testbed, the three
interfaces decide how a demand shift is weathered, not how the service performs
once the shift has passed.

The campaigns also separate failure modes that monitoring and scaling are often
discussed as one. Across the telemetry modes, missing summaries and late
summaries are different faults. The first cost about nine times the episode
tail with complete separation, and the second cost in proportion to the margin
it consumed. In the action interface, execution and benefit are separate. The
storage action relieved its tier on the mixed press, where that tier was loaded
but not binding, and users gained little, while the compute action left the
pressed tier worse than no scaling on the data-access press. In the admission
interface, latency and reliability are separate, and one choice sets both at
once. The mode fills the routing pool, so a lost message hides ready capacity
from it and a premature message exposes capacity that cannot yet absorb
traffic.

The interfaces also differ in the control records they leave behind, but not
in the controller cost that carries them: across the three campaigns the
controller's memory stayed within a narrow band, at 66--77~MB in every
configuration, and its CPU within 3.7--24~\%; the delivery,
check, and action records differed as designed (medians per configuration in
Table~\ref{tab:app_config_control_cost}).

Every finding above holds for the evaluated two-domain testbed, its workloads,
and its resource limits; the limitations of the evaluation are detailed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.


\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work}\label{ch:conclusions}
This chapter answers the research questions and sets out the contributions,
limitations, and future research.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
This dissertation examined three interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain
and established, for each, which property of the crossing decides the
outcome and under which conditions: telemetry consumption by the controller,
the tier served by the scaling action, and the admission of a ready backend
to traffic. Each interface was varied on its own, on one platform, in its own
campaign. Table~\ref{tab:ch6_answers} summarises the answer to each research
question.

Read together, the three answers have a common shape, developed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The interfaces act on the transition, not on the
steady state. These findings hold for the evaluated two-domain testbed, its
workloads, and its resource limits. No aggregate cost along the chain is
claimed, because the campaigns vary one interface at a time under different
workloads and their segments are not additive
(Section~\ref{sec:lit_synthesis}).

The motivations of Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation} close as findings or
explicit non-claims. The storage tier behaved as a live capacity that must
follow demand, and the storage action reached users only when the press bound
that tier (RQ2). Scale-up alone did not guarantee usable capacity instantly:
the telemetry mode set when new capacity became usable, and the admission
mode set when a ready backend began serving (RQ1 and RQ3). The crossings
differed in more than latency: missing evidence, a mismatched tier, and an
unverified readiness message each produced a distinct failure (RQ1, RQ2, and
RQ3). The replication bandwidth consumed by a membership change was not
metered and is not a measured claim.

\section{Research Contributions}\label{sec:contributions_revisited}
The contributions stated in Section~\ref{sec:contributions} are supported as
follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Telemetry modes (RQ1).} The four modes were compared on an
    otherwise identical platform, separating the two faults they carry.
    Missing intermediate evidence cost in every replicate relative to push,
    while a complete but delayed stream cost conditionally, in proportion to
    the absorption margin it consumed
    (Section~\ref{sec:results_rq1_delivery}).

    \item \emph{Bottleneck-aware scaling action (RQ2).} Scaling the tier under
    pressure was compared with two fixed single-tier policies and with no
    scaling under two demand episodes. The matched action relieved the pressed
    tier and reached users, a mismatched action did not, and the
    bottleneck-aware policy gave the highest completion under both episodes
    and was the only one that spent on both tiers
    (Section~\ref{sec:results_rq2_action}).

    \item \emph{Readiness admission (RQ3).} The four admission modes were
    crossed with three message conditions, measuring the admission step that
    the placement literature leaves outside its scope: a bounded delay while
    the platform is healthy, an all-or-nothing loss when the message is
    dropped without a check, and damage confined to the modes that admit on a
    premature message (Section~\ref{sec:results_rq3_readiness}).

    \item \emph{Comparative synthesis.} The three interfaces were synthesised
    under one classification, robust, conditional, or not established within
    each campaign, with no quantity pooled across campaigns
    (Table~\ref{tab:synthesis}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
The architecture and the experiments share one trade-off. To vary an interface
in isolation, telemetry consumption, the scaling decision, and routing had to
run in one controller process, on one host, under one configuration of
everything else. That choice is what makes the measured effects attributable to
the interface under test, and it is also what bounds them. The limitations
below follow from it.

The first cost is that the platform has no external baseline. Because the three
functions are co-located, the evaluation compares configurations of one
controller process per domain. It does not compare against an orchestration in
which monitoring, autoscaling, and load balancing run as separate processes or
on separate nodes, so the cost of that separation is not measured, and the
results cannot be read as a comparison against a conventional orchestrator.
Co-location also removes distribution from the deployment: the testbed is a
single host with two emulated domains, the control plane is host-local, and the
data path is single-site, with no introduced latency or jitter between the
controller and the resources it manages. No multi-site or wide-area claim is
made, and the compute tier is a set of Docker containers rather than a cluster
orchestrator, so scheduling, image distribution, and multi-node placement were
not evaluated. Finally, co-location concentrates risk. The controller keeps its
state in memory and has no leader election or failover, so a restart loses the
routing-pool state until it is re-derived from the network. Its packet path runs
inline in the OpenFlow event loop, so throughput under high connection churn was
not characterised, and controller failure, recovery, peer-state expiry, and
secure multi-domain control were not evaluated.

The second cost is in what the routing layer can observe. The routing pool is
rebuilt on every topology tick rather than atomically on registration, so a
backend can be recorded as admitted before it is selectable, and a draining
backend can remain selectable until the next rebuild. At a one-second tick, the
admission-to-first-request interval carries an up-to-one-second boundary. That
boundary limits the precision of the admission timestamp, not the reported
contrasts, which are several seconds apart, but it means the admission instant
is a registration instant and not a routability instant. Routing is also
connection-level, so an existing flow keeps its backend until it expires or is
renewed, and a scaling action cannot redistribute traffic already in flight.

The third cost is in what the storage action can be asked to do. MongoDB
accepts writes only on the replica-set primary, so storage scale-up adds read
capacity and the evaluation covers read-bound storage pressure. The action
activates a reserve prepared before the reaction path, so the measured action
excludes cold member creation and initial synchronisation, and a deployment that
creates a member on demand would see a longer and more variable action. The
action is a membership change in the domain's own replica set, so cross-domain
placement and the Tier~0 and Tier~1 data paths were held constant. Holding the
other interfaces constant has a cost of its own: each campaign varies one
interface and fixes the other two, so the effects are measured in isolation and
their interactions are not measured.

Within these bounds the trade-off holds. Each interface was varied alone over
seed-matched blocks, on a platform whose other two interfaces and whose demand
were fixed, and that is what allows an effect to be attributed to the interface
under test. The bounds mark where the findings stop rather than where they
weaken, and each opens a direction in Section~\ref{sec:future_work}.

\section{Future Research Directions}\label{sec:future_work}
Each direction below follows from one of the bounds set out in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\emph{Compound treatments across interfaces.} The campaigns vary one interface
and fix the other two, so the effects are measured apart. An injection that
delays several handoffs at once, with a configurable delay at each, would test
whether the effects compose (Section~\ref{sec:lit_synthesis}).

\emph{An external baseline.} With the three functions co-located, the platform
has no separate-process orchestration to compare against. Deploying monitoring,
autoscaling, and routing as separate processes and repeating a campaign would
measure the cost of that separation. Repeating the telemetry campaign over
off-the-shelf observability components, such as an OpenTelemetry collector over
a message broker, would show whether the measured effects are properties of the
modes or of this implementation, and varying the window size in the same setting
would trade freshness against noise.

\emph{Admission as routability.} Admission is recorded at registration while the
routing pool follows the topology tick, and an existing flow keeps its backend
until it expires or is renewed. Updating the pool atomically on registration,
and rebalancing in-flight connections when a scaling action fires, would make
the admission timestamp a routability timestamp and let a scaling action reach
traffic that is already running.

\emph{Storage placement and tiering.} Only the Tier~2 membership change in the
domain's own replica set was evaluated, and only with a reserve prepared before
the reaction path. Evaluating Tier~0 and Tier~1, adding cold member creation to
the reaction path, and instrumenting replica-sync bandwidth and the transient
cost of a membership change would characterise the storage side of the chain and
extend the storage action beyond read capacity.

\emph{Action policy beyond the evaluated envelope.} The storage action was
evaluated under read-bound pressure. A write-bound press would test the other
side of the primary-only limit, a wrong-tier action would price its user cost,
and regime shifts beyond the tested demand would show where a residual benefit
is expected.

\emph{Scale, distribution, and realism.} A larger deployment across separate
hosts or sites, real traffic traces, and ML-driven threshold adaptation would
test how far the findings extend beyond a single-host testbed. Controller
redundancy, peer-state expiry, and secure multi-domain control would remove the
control-plane single point of failure. A programmable data plane such as P4
would move steering and telemetry into the network, at the cost of reproducing
the connection-tracking behaviour in-switch.
