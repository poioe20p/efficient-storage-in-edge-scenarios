\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:introduction} %DONE
Edge web services that encompass monitoring, auto-scaling and routing are
commonly built from separate components, each with its own policies. 
How well such a service absorbs a
change in demand therefore depends not only on what each component does
individually, but also on the interfaces between them. Coordination across those
interfaces can fall short, and where it does, orchestration efficiency and
reliability can degrade and may reduce the quality of service users
experience~\parencite{Bogdanov2018Kurma,Podolskiy2018IaaSReactiveAutoscalingPerformanceChallenges}.

The remainder of this chapter is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation} presents the context, motivation and
problem statement; Section~\ref{sec:objectives} states the objectives;
Section~\ref{sec:research_questions} lists the research questions;
Section~\ref{sec:research_methodology} details the methodology used to
develop the thesis; Section~\ref{sec:contributions} summarises the main
contributions; and Section~\ref{sec:document_structure} outlines the rest
of the dissertation.

\section{Context, Motivation, and Problem Statement}\label{sec:context_motivation}
In 2025, global internet traffic across fixed and mobile networks exceeded
7 000 exabytes\footnote{One exabyte equals \(10^{9}\) gigabytes.}, more than
doubling since 2020~\parencite{ITU2025InternetTraffic}, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:internet_traffic}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/global-internet-traffic-over-the-years.pdf}
    \caption{Global internet traffic between 2020 and 2025. Source: Statista, based on ITU data~\parencite{ITU2025InternetTraffic}.}
    \label{fig:internet_traffic}
\end{figure}

    This growth has been driven by many technological advancements, some of
which have in turn spurred the emergence of real-time interactive and
data-intensive applications, among others. Examples include web streaming
and cloud gaming, both services where users expect sub-second latency and
high-resolution multimedia, highlighting the need for fast and capable
data processing.

Cloud computing has been the backbone that enables these applications to
operate at scale. It offers automatic scaling, both horizontally
and vertically, a pay-per-use model and, among other aspects, a
distributed offering and availability that have established it as the
foundational layer supporting modern applications and services
\parencite{Gurung2026CloudRevolutionTracingOriginsRise}.

    Yet, in parallel, a growing share of internet traffic now originates from
mobile devices, which have become the main gateway to internet access
\parencite{Gaudiaut2026MobileTrafficShare}. Much of this access is
therefore generated and consumed at the network edge. Edge computing
brings computational and storage resources closer to the end users,
allowing for much lower latency in delivering services and
applications~\parencite{Cao2020OverviewEdgeComputingResearch,Satyanarayanan2017EmergenceEdgeComputing}.
Since data can be processed and stored locally, only a subset of it is
sent to the cloud~\parencite{Cao2020OverviewEdgeComputingResearch}. 

    Despite these advantages, edge resources are dispersed, which translates
into less capacity per location and greater complexity in managing the
distributed edge servers. As Satyanarayanan puts it, \enquote{the dispersion inherent in
edge computing raises the complexity of management considerably}
\parencite[p.~37]{Satyanarayanan2017EmergenceEdgeComputing}. This complexity is
aggravated by considerable variation in user demand over time and by the
heterogeneous resource requirements of the requests served. Luo et al. further
highlight that, without efficient resource scheduling, this distributed nature
leads to resource waste
\parencite{Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}. Resource
management therefore becomes increasingly important, since the limited
capacity of each location must be adjusted over time to suit the constant
fluctuation of user demand.

    The resource management problem gains more relevance when the services
themselves are stateful. Unlike stateless microservices, which can be
replicated freely and absorb traffic immediately, a stateful edge service
depends on data held close to the compute, so its storage tier is itself
a live capacity that must follow demand alongside the compute tier.
Making that capacity usable takes time, since a new storage unit must
synchronise before it can serve reads, and maintaining the replicated
copies consumes storage and replication bandwidth
\parencite{Breitbach2019ContextAwareDataTaskPlacement}.

    Scale-up alone does not guarantee usable capacity instantly. The
orchestration must detect the demand shift, deliver that information to the
decision point, provision the right kind of capacity, and redirect traffic
to it. The steps hand over to one another across boundaries between
functions, and what the next step acts on can be late, incomplete,
mismatched, or unverified.

    This sequence forms the demand-to-capacity chain, the path that an
orchestration system takes to convert a change in overall user demand into
capacity that serves that demand. A demand
shift is first observed by monitoring and telemetry, which deliver the
evidence to a decision point. The decision point selects a scaling action,
such as scaling compute or scaling storage, and the action provisions new
capacity. Finally the new capacity attains a readiness state and is admitted
to traffic, which is the point at which it becomes usable. The chain is therefore a
timeline through the orchestration system, and its steps are joined by
interfaces.\footnote{Throughout this dissertation, the
\emph{demand-to-capacity chain} denotes the full timeline from a demand
shift to usable capacity. An \emph{interface} is a boundary between two
steps of the chain at which two functions meet, and what crosses an
interface is the evidence, the command, or the state that the next step
acts on. A \emph{handoff delay} is the latency between a function's handover and the
next step acting on it. The reviewed literature addresses this topic
mainly at boundaries between functions that run under independent control
loops, each observing, deciding, and acting on its own. The properties of
what crosses (the completeness and freshness of the evidence, the match
between the command and the demand, and the preservation and verification
of a state claim) are studied here one at a time. The \emph{coordination gap} is the
accumulated effect along the chain, which this dissertation does not
measure as a whole.} The three interfaces between its steps, highlighted in
Figure~\ref{fig:demand_capacity_chain}, are the subject of this thesis.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/demand_to_capacity_chain_v2.png}
    \caption{The demand-to-capacity chain: from a demand shift to usable
    capacity, with the three interfaces studied in this dissertation
    highlighted.}
    \label{fig:demand_capacity_chain}
\end{figure}

    The steps described above must be coordinated under the time pressure of
an ongoing demand shift. In practice, however, they are commonly performed by
separate components. A monitoring system collects metrics, an
auto-scaler provisions capacity, and a load balancer redirects traffic,
each running under its own control loop and acting on its own view of the
service. In the reviewed literature, the cost of these crossings is
acknowledged but never isolated or varied as an independent
variable~\parencite{Qu2018AutoScalingWebApplicationsClouds,Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.
The crossings also differ in more than latency: evidence can arrive
complete and fresh, complete but delayed, or with intermediate observations
missing~\parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork};
a capacity command can serve the tier that binds the demand or one that
does not~\parencite{Nikravesh2017ImpactDatabaseLayerAutoScaling}; and a
readiness claim can be verified before admission, trusted before it is
true, or lost~\parencite{Neghabi2018LoadBalancingMechanismsSoftwareDefined}. 
This thesis isolates the three interfaces and measures the
effect of each of these properties on its own. The platform built for this
purpose is organised as two peer \ac{SDN} controllers, one per network domain.
Within each controller the consuming functions of the chain (telemetry consumption, scaling
decisions, and traffic routing) run in the same process, so that each
studied interface can be configured and varied on its own rather than
observed as an incidental coupling. Telemetry
production and aggregation remain external to the controller, making the
delivery of aggregated demand evidence an explicit boundary, while
scaling and admission are exposed as lifecycle hooks.

    Along this chain, three interfaces are studied in a stateful service
deployed across two network domains: how completely and how fresh demand
evidence reaches the controller (telemetry delivery semantics); whether
the capacity command serves the tier the demand presses on
(bottleneck-aware scaling action); and whether a ready backend is verified
before it is admitted to traffic (readiness admission).

    The thesis does not claim that this SDN-based architecture is superior
to existing orchestration platforms such as Kubernetes or NFV Management
and Orchestration (MANO) platforms, nor that its mechanisms generalise
beyond the tested infrastructure. It claims only that these three interfaces 
are measurable in isolation and that measurement can establish which property
of each interface decides the outcome, and under what conditions. The reviewed
literature has not previously characterised them this way, and no
aggregate cost along the chain is claimed.

\section{Objectives}\label{sec:objectives} %DONE
The main objective of this thesis is to contribute to more efficient
resource orchestration for stateful edge services by characterising the
interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain under controlled demand regimes,
and by establishing, for each interface, which property of the crossing
decides the outcome and under which conditions: how complete and how fresh
the demand evidence is, whether the scaling action serves the tier the
demand presses on, and whether the readiness claim is preserved and
verified before a new backend is admitted.

    The platform keeps controller-side coordination in-process while
retaining telemetry delivery as an explicit, configurable boundary. Along
with explicit lifecycle hooks for scaling and admission, this arrangement
exposes the selected interfaces to direct measurement. Because the
components share one process and are therefore coupled, making each
interface independently configurable is itself a deliberate design choice,
so that the platform can vary the delivery of demand evidence, the scaling
action selected, or the admission of ready capacity, and measure the effect.

\section{Research Questions}\label{sec:research_questions} %DONE
This thesis seeks to answer the following three research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{RQ1} -- How do three ways of delivering demand evidence
    to the controller, more specifically, event-preserving, delayed event-preserving, and
    lossy delivery (latest-state polling and sampled push), affect what the
    controller observes about overload,
    how it responds by scaling, and the quality of service users
    experience during a demand shift?

    \item \textit{RQ2} -- Under compute-bound and data-access-bound demand,
    how does bottleneck-aware scale-up, compared with fixed single-tier
    policies, affect targeted-tier relief, service recovery, and resource use?

    \item \textit{RQ3} -- When a newly provisioned backend becomes ready,
    how does the readiness-admission mechanism, direct lifecycle
    notification versus periodic discovery, affect when the backend
    actually serves requests, and what does the admission evidence
    guarantee when spare capacity is already low and the readiness claim
    is lost or premature (delivered before the backend can serve)?
\end{itemize}

\section{Research Methodology}\label{sec:research_methodology} %DONE
The work presented in this dissertation follows the Design Science Research
(DSR) methodology \parencite{Hevner2004DesignScienceInformationSystemsResearch1},
\parencite{vomBrocke2020IntroductionDesignScienceResearch}
proposed in \parencite{Peffers2007DesignScienceResearchMethodologyInformation},
and demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:design_science_research_methodology}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{images/Design Science Research process model.png}
    \caption{Design Science Research Methodology}
    \label{fig:design_science_research_methodology}    
\end{figure}

    Design science produces and evaluates purposeful artefacts that address certain
real-world problems, the research contribution corresponds to the knowledge gained
by designing the artefact and evaluating it against defined objectives.
In \ac{DSRM} the research is organised into six categories: problem
identification and motivation, definition of the objectives of a solution,
design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication.

    This dissertation follows a problem-centred approach, in which the problem
motivates the research and is defined as the coordination between the
functions that orchestrate an edge service, where handovers that are late,
incomplete, mismatched, or unverified can lower orchestration efficiency
and the quality of service users experience. The problem definition is grounded in the
state of the art reviewed in Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}. The six
activities unfolded as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Problem identification and motivation.} Edge services whose
monitoring, scaling and routing modules operate as independent components
depend on information that crosses the boundaries between them, and how
those crossings are operated can degrade orchestration
efficiency. The problem is introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}, and the review of the state of the art in
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review} establishes both that these functions are typically
studied in isolation and that single-process architectures with independently
configurable interfaces are less commonly present in the reviewed literature, which
motivates and delimits the problem.

\item \textit{Objectives of a solution.} As defined in Section~\ref{sec:objectives},
the solution is an experimental platform, not a novel algorithm, whose
purpose is to make the interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain
measurable, so that their impact on resource orchestration can be
evaluated through measurement and better understood.

\item \textit{Design and development.} The artefact is the single-process platform
described in Chapters~\ref{ch:system_architecture} and~\ref{ch:implementation}: two peer
SDN controllers (OS-Ken/OpenFlow), one per network domain, each hosting
controller-side telemetry consumption, elasticity decision-making and the
double-VIP routing model, with Docker containerised service backends and a
MongoDB replica set per domain serving as the storage tier, each scaled in and
out through MongoDB native replication. The
technology choices are justified by
their utility for the platform. SDN provides a single programmable control
plane through which routing behaviour can be re-programmed directly in a central manner, 
Docker enables fast provisioning and teardown of service backends, and MongoDB provides native
replica-set replication with concurrent access.

\item \textit{Demonstration.} The artefact was deployed as a running system in
an emulated edge environment with two network domains, and was driven with
scheduled workload phases reproducing demand shifts in order to demonstrate that
the platform operates end-to-end from demand
observation through scaling action to traffic admission, with the tested
interface varied across runs of each experimental campaign for evaluation.

\item \textit{Evaluation.} Each research question was evaluated in a dedicated
experimental campaign reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. The evaluation
follows a pre-registered measurement and statistical procedure. A campaign is
the set of independent runs that answers one research question by varying one
interface of the demand-to-capacity chain while the others are held fixed;
each run executes the campaign's workload and is treated as an experimental
unit. The workloads are issued by a scheduled, open-loop workload driver,
which applies the offered request schedule independently of the responses it
receives, so that the offered load is preserved even when the service
degrades. The primary comparisons are detailed in
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}. Internal, external and repeatability
validity threats are discussed in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\item \textit{Communication.} The communication of the problem, the artefact
and the findings is accomplished through this dissertation itself and its
public defence, as well as through any resulting publications.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Main Contributions of this Dissertation}\label{sec:contributions} %DONE
This dissertation makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item An experimental characterisation of telemetry delivery semantics
    (event-preserving, delayed event-preserving, and lossy delivery) and of
    their effects on overload observability, scaling response, and transient
    service quality (RQ1).
    \item An experimental characterisation of bottleneck-aware scaling
    action selection, more specifically how deciding between
    compute and storage scale-up
    from the observed bottleneck, compared with fixed single-tier policies,
    affects targeted-tier relief, service recovery, and resource use under
    compute-bound and data-access-bound demand (RQ2).
    \item An experimental characterisation of readiness admission that
    quantifies, under direct lifecycle notification versus periodic
    discovery (including a bounded fallback variant), the time from
    verified readiness to routable and usable capacity. It further
    prices two failure modes: the reliability cost the faster mechanism
    incurs when its readiness notification is lost and no bounded
    re-derivation recovers it, with spare capacity scarce, and the
    exposure of the trusting rules to a premature readiness claim, which
    the probing rules absorb (RQ3).
    \item A comparative synthesis of the observation, action-selection, and
    readiness-admission interfaces under their respective controlled
    workload regimes, identifying which effects were robust,
    conditional, or not established.
\end{enumerate}

    These contributions are claimed within the evaluated two-domain testbed
and workload families.

\section{Dissertation Structure}\label{sec:document_structure} %DONE
This dissertation is organised into six chapters:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Chapter 1} introduces the research, presenting its context, motivation and
problem statement, the objectives, the research questions that emerged, the methodology that
guided the work, and the main contributions.

\item \textit{Chapter 2} reviews the background and related work. After presenting the
methodology used to conduct the review, it surveys the platform technologies on which the

admission on SDN, and resource orchestration on SDN, examines the default separation of
monitoring, scaling and routing
into independent components observed across these areas, and concludes with the research
gaps that motivate this dissertation.

\item \textit{Chapter 3} presents the architecture and design of the proposed solution for
edge services: the design requirements, the SDN-based architecture, the distributed elastic
allocation of compute and storage resources, the monitoring and decision engine, and the control
workflow.

\item \textit{Chapter 4} describes the implementation of the proposed solution: the
experimental infrastructure, the implementation of the elastic allocation of compute and storage
resources, the monitoring and decision engine, the control workflow, and the validation of the
implementation.

\item \textit{Chapter 5} presents the experimental results. Following the
demand-to-capacity chain, the experimental campaigns address telemetry delivery
semantics (RQ1), bottleneck-aware scaling action selection (RQ2), and readiness admission
to traffic (RQ3), followed by a synthesis across the three interfaces.

\item \textit{Chapter 6} concludes the dissertation, restating the findings and the
contributions, discussing the limitations of the work, and outlining directions for future
research.
\end{itemize}

\chapter{Background and Related Work}\label{ch:literature_review} %DONE
This chapter presents the background and related work that motivated
the research, selected through the search strategy described in
Section~\ref{sec:review_method}. The review is separated into two parts.
First, the technologies and concepts used to build the experimental
platform are explained and surveyed, each introduced for what it is
and why it suits the edge scenario. These are, more specifically,
edge computing, SDN and OpenFlow, containerisation, and NoSQL
document stores with a focus on MongoDB.

    The layer that binds these technologies and concepts into a
running service, known as resource orchestration, is also reviewed, alongside the
three functions it covers, which are monitoring and telemetry, auto-scaling,
and load balancing. Then the focus is drawn onto the 
interfaces between them that are part of the demand-to-capacity chain to evaluate
how demand shifts are detected,
which scaling action is selected when several are available, and how
readiness admission is handled. 
    
    Within each area, the review examines whether and how the interfaces
between these functions are considered, studied, evaluated, or mentioned.
It asks whether the reviewed work provides evidence that delay, loss,
mismatched action, or unverified state at these handoffs causes
orchestration or service-quality problems, and what ideas each field
offers about these crossings, with the aim of establishing a solid ground
for the evaluation of the three interfaces of the demand-to-capacity
chain. 

    This examination grounds the claim, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation},
that these interfaces are acknowledged in the literature but have not been
systematically isolated and studied. The orchestration
platforms that provide the context for these functions are reviewed in
Section~\ref{sec:lit_sdn_orchestration}, and the findings across all areas,
together with the research gaps they define, are synthesised in
Section~\ref{sec:lit_synthesis}.

This section presents and explains the four building blocks that define
the testbed evaluated in this thesis, edge computing, SDN with OpenFlow,
containerisation, and MongoDB.

How the building blocks are applied

\subsection{Edge Computing} %DONE
Edge computing is the placement of computational and storage resources
between end devices and centralised cloud data centres, close to where
data is produced and consumed. It complements rather than replaces the
cloud, as latency-sensitive processing is shifted to the edge,
while large-scale analytics and long-term
storage remain in the cloud
\parencite{Cao2020OverviewEdgeComputingResearch}.

    In essence, edge computing exists because centralised clouds
alone cannot serve every workload efficiently, and bringing computation
closer to the user lowers the latency of
interactive and data-intensive services, and processing and storing 
data locally reduces the ingress bandwidth into the cloud
\parencite{Satyanarayanan2017EmergenceEdgeComputing}.

    Despite the advantages of edge computing, as mentioned previously in
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}, the inherent dispersion of edge resources
increases their management and orchestration complexity.
Resource heterogeneity, scarcity, and demand varying over time are all 
aspects that can degrade resource orchestration efficiency, making it a 
concept of continuous study and research
\parencite{Liu2019SurveyEdgeComputingSystemsTools,Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}.
Scheduling that does not follow the workload leads either to
over-provisioning, where resources are held but not used, or to
under-provisioning, where service quality degrades
\parencite{Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}.
Luo et al. also identify that the joint scheduling of
communication, computing, and storage resources is an open
direction for the field
\parencite{Luo2021ResourceSchedulingEdgeComputingSurvey}.

    Managing these platforms remains an open challenge, and their
combination with SDN is recorded as an area with room for further
investigation
\parencite{Nain2024ResourceOptimizationEdgeSDNEdge}. The service
evaluated in this dissertation runs in an emulated edge environment as
a stateful edge service whose capacity must follow fluctuating demand,
which is the management problem the remainder of this chapter reviews.

\subsection{SDN and OpenFlow} %DONE
Software-Defined Networking separates the control plane, which decides
how traffic is handled, from the data plane, which forwards packets, so
that forwarding behaviour can be reprogrammed centrally from a
controller instead of being configured device by device. 

    Baktir et al. survey how edge computing benefits from this decoupling
and organise the gains into benefit areas such as high-resolution
control built on a centralised view and programmatic access, arguing
that SDN can act as the enabler that lowers the complexity barriers
and enhances the flexibility of edge deployments \parencite{Baktir2017HowCanEdgeComputingBenefit}. 

    The OpenFlow protocol implements the decoupling through match-action
flow rules installed on the switches, and is, as reviewed by Waseem et al.,
the most extensively used SDN protocol in the research community
\parencite{Waseem2022SoftwareDefinedNetworkingSDNReview}. 

    In its OpenFlow form, a single controller re-programs the forwarding
behaviour of the network by installing and updating match-action flow rules,
so that network behaviour can follow the life cycle of the services it
carries without reconfiguring the forwarding devices individually. The
decoupling is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:sdn_architecture}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/sdn_architecture_v3.png}
    \caption{Software-Defined Networking architecture: the control plane is
    decoupled from the data plane and programs the forwarding devices through
    the OpenFlow protocol.}
    \label{fig:sdn_architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Containerisation and Docker} %DONE
Containers package an application with its dependencies and share the
host kernel, which makes them lightweight and quick to start up compared
to virtual machines, properties that suit resource-constrained edge
platforms. In this thesis Docker is adopted as the platform's
containerisation tool because of its wide adoption for building and
running containers.

\subsection{NoSQL and MongoDB}\label{sec:platform_storage} %DONE
Document-oriented NoSQL stores organise data as schema-flexible
documents rather than as rows of relational tables. Because related
data is embedded in a single document and the model avoids joins and
fixed schemas, records can be partitioned and replicated across
servers, which is how these stores scale horizontally, whereas
relational systems are traditionally scaled vertically by enlarging a
single server. This model suits the heterogeneous data of edge and IoT
services, whose structure varies between sources and evolves over time.

    MongoDB is a document store of this family. It holds data as BSON
documents within collections and provides a flexible schema.
In MongoDB, redundancy is organised natively around the replica
set, a group of \texttt{mongod} processes that maintain the same
data set. One member is elected primary and serves the clients'
writes, which it records in an operation log, the remaining
members, the secondaries, replicate by applying that log
asynchronously and autonomously. Members exchange heartbeats,
and when the primary fails or becomes unreachable,
the secondaries hold an election and
promote one of their own, so the set keeps serving without
operator intervention~\parencite{MongoDBDocsReplicaSet}. Members can
also be added to and removed
from a deployment while it keeps serving, which turns the replica
set into a controllable unit of storage capacity rather than a
fixed configuration. Figure~\ref{fig:mongodb_replica_set} shows the
members of a set and the replication paths between them.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/mongodb_replica_set_v3.png}
    \caption{MongoDB replica set: a primary member serving the clients and
    secondary members to which the data is replicated.}
    \label{fig:mongodb_replica_set}
\end{figure}

    This dissertation builds on that capability. The storage tier is
scaled by adding and removing replica-set members while the set keeps
serving, leveraging MongoDB's native replication.

\section{Monitoring \& Telemetry}\label{sec:lit_monitoring} % DONE
Monitoring and telemetry observe the state of a running service and
deliver that evidence to whoever acts on it. Monitoring is the
collection of resource and load metrics, telemetry the transport that
carries the collected data to the consumer, and together they form the
first link of the demand-to-capacity chain. Every later decision, on
scaling or on routing, can act only on the evidence this link delivers,
at the time it delivers it, and with whatever intermediate observations
the delivery process preserves.
    In edge deployments the link matters even more, because the
evidence is produced across the service nodes and must reach the decision
point,
so any delay or gap in its delivery becomes delay or degradation in the
orchestration response. 

    The state of the art in this area is built around a pipeline in which
per-node collectors sample resource and load metrics, an
aggregation layer summarises the samples, and the resulting series feed
the consumers that make decisions. In orchestrators this pipeline is
standardised around periodic scraping into a time-series store, as in
OSM-based NFV for transparent VNF clusters, where the MANO collector
polls the infrastructure into a Prometheus database that the control
modules consume
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.

    Delivery is conventionally pull-based, the
consumer requesting the latest sample at a fixed interval
\parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork}, and this
choice alone determines much of what the consumer can know. Between two
collection instants the monitored state is unobserved, and what the
pipeline forwards between instants determines whether the consumer
receives the complete history, a complete but delayed history, or only
part of it. These different forms are defined
as event-preserving delivery, delayed event-preserving delivery,
and lossy delivery (latest-state polling and sampled push).
Their effect on resource orchestration
is part of the subject of study in this thesis.

    The reviewed evidence spans three contexts in which this link has
been studied: cluster-federation monitoring, network telemetry, and
edge-platform observability. In each, the review asks whether the
delivery mechanism itself is varied, what downstream consequence the
variation is shown to produce, and what the work leaves as a given.

    Within cluster-federation monitoring, the collection interval has
been shown to be a genuine variable rather than a fixed parameter. AdapPF
\parencite{Huang2023AdapPFSelfAdaptiveScrapeInterval} varies the
Prometheus scrape interval of a geo-distributed Kubernetes federation and
measures the effect on a downstream consumer. Under high load, a coarse
sixty-second interval substantially degrades scheduling accuracy relative
to a five-second interval, and an adaptive cadence restores it while
reducing cross-cluster monitoring traffic.
AdapPF is the only reviewed work that varies monitoring freshness and
measures a downstream consequence, although not
in the context of a stateful service. 

Zhou and Yong study what to monitor rather than how it is delivered, comparing
custom-metric autoscaling driven by application-level 5xx error rates
against conventional CPU-based autoscaling in Kubernetes, and find that
metric choice changes scaling quality under load, albeit on a small
single-service testbed \parencite{Zhou2024ImplementHPANginxServiceUsing}.

    A second context, network telemetry, is likewise pull-based.
Yaseen reviews programmable network-wide monitoring and records that
SNMP polling produces
\enquote{visibility gaps} between collection instants, calling for
monitoring to be \enquote{tightly integrated with network control and
automation} \parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork}.
Hung et al. present an SDN controller for 5G backhaul networks that
closes a telemetry-and-routing loop, with telemetry collection
delegated to decentralised microservices reached through an API,
showing that monitoring and control functions can be combined at one
control point, although the loop omits scaling altogether
\parencite{Hung2022SDNControllerMonitoringArchitecture5G}.

    At the platform level, adjacent observability
work exhibits the same open dimension. Usman et al., surveying
observability of distributed edge and container-based microservices,
leave the definition of appropriate measurement intervals as an
open research topic
\parencite{Usman2022SurveyObservabilityDistributedEdgeAmp}.
None of these works runs over a stateful service, so they are read
here for the delivery mechanisms they expose, not as direct precedents
for the platform under study.

    Across the reviewed literature, therefore, the building blocks of the
delivery question appear separately. AdapPF varies the interval that
produces delay, and Yaseen documents the gaps that polling leaves, yet
no reviewed work distinguishes delayed event-preserving delivery from
lossy delivery, and none traces either regime through a stateful
service from observation to user-visible consequence. This distinction is the gap
this dissertation isolates and measures.

\section{Auto Scaling}\label{sec:lit_auto_scaling} %DONE
Auto-scaling adjusts the amount of allocated resources to the load that
a service receives. When demand grows, additional capacity is
provisioned, and when demand recedes, surplus capacity is released, so
that the service keeps serving requests without holding resources it
does not use. Auto-scaling strategies are mostly grouped into
horizontal scaling, which changes the number of units, and vertical
scaling, which changes the capacity of a single unit, with hybrid
strategies combining the two
\parencite{ElMrabet2025ComprehensiveSurveyTaxonomyAutoscalingTechniques}.
Horizontal scaling suits the edge scenario, and is the direction this
dissertation follows. Throughout this dissertation, adding a unit of
capacity to a tier is referred to as scale-up, and removing one as
scale-down. 

    In edge computing, a site's capacity is bounded by the nodes deployed
there, so enlarging an individual unit has a hard ceiling, whereas
adjusting the number of units lets capacity grow and shrink as demand
changes. When and how many resources to add or remove therefore
determines both the quality of service during a demand shift and
the efficiency with which scarce resources are managed.

    Qu et al. classify the auto-scalers along several characteristics,
among them the scaling indicator, the resource estimation method, and
the timing of the action, and Taherizadeh and Stankovski present a
taxonomy for edge computing whose dimensions include adaptation
intervals and architectural support for multi-tier applications, noting
that a scaling approach should account for different dynamic
provisioning policies at each tier. Both surveys take the
scaling of service instances, whether virtual machines or containers,
as the unit of analysis. The main questions the field asks are when to
scale and how many instances to add, which kind of capacity is
provisioned, when applicable, is not among the classification dimensions
\parencite{Qu2018AutoScalingWebApplicationsClouds,Taherizadeh2017AutoScalingApplicationsEdgeComputing}.

    With SDN, the traffic side is added, but the scaled
resource remains the one the service function is built from.
Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. couple SDN-based traffic steering with
horizontal scaling of virtualised network functions, so that the SDN
controllers re-program the forwarding paths while instances of the same
pre-dimensioned function pool are activated or deactivated. The decision
reduces to whether and how many instances of that one function to
activate.\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.
In a stateful edge service, however, the storage tier is
itself a live capacity. When the pressure sits in the data rather than
in the function instances, adding instances does not relieve the storage tier.

    Predictive and learning-based approaches sharpen the same decision
without widening it. Toka et al. formalise the Kubernetes horizontal
autoscaler's control model to anticipate demand for edge clusters
\parencite{Toka2021MachineLearningScalingManagementKubernetes}, Ju et
al. and Cheng et al. forecast workload to adjust replica counts ahead of
demand, the latter jointly with placement
\parencite{Ju2021ProactiveAutoscalingEdgeComputingSystems,Cheng2023ProScaleProactiveAutoscalingMicroserviceTime},
and Li et al. couple scaling and placement in a single optimisation
\parencite{Li2021JointOptimizationAutoScalingAdaptive}. Across these
systems the optimisation lies on the forecast and adjusting the number of
compute replicas over time. The improvement pursued is a better forecast of
how many instances to run, which tier holds the limiting resource does
not enter the scope of study.

    Scaling one resource does not always make the service better,
because the pressure may sit elsewhere. Nikravesh et al. quantify what
that default costs in a three-tier web service. The scaling decisions
taken for the business tier turn out to be insensitive to database
capacity, yet reducing database capacity raises the rate of SLA
violations, so service quality can be governed by the tier that the
scaling decisions ignore
\parencite{Nikravesh2017ImpactDatabaseLayerAutoScaling}. Dogani et
al. make the same observation in their survey, noting that adding
resources does not necessarily raise throughput when the bottleneck
lies elsewhere
\parencite{Dogani2023AutoScalingTechniquesContainerCloud}. Among the
systems covered by that survey, only a few take cross-service effects
into account. Waterfall, a machine-learning auto-scaler, accounts
for how scaling one microservice can shift the bottleneck towards
downstream services. The mechanism remains compute-side, and no
surveyed system selects a storage action.

    PBScaler identifies the microservice whose degradation
propagates through the invocation graph, ranks candidate
bottlenecks, and optimises replica management around the
identified bottleneck
\parencite{Xie2024PBScalerBottleneckAwareAutoscalingFramework}.
Bottleneck awareness therefore exists in the reviewed state of the art,
but the bottleneck it locates is always a microservice and the action it
takes is always replica scaling, so the selection between
compute and storage capacity remains outside the action space.

    The closest reviewed precedent to selecting between resource tiers
is the provisioning model of Li et al., which adds both cloud compute
instances and data replicas in cloud-assisted edge systems, from a
forecast of future workload built over historical workload data
\parencite{Li2020HeterogeneityAwareElasticProvisioningCloud}. Both
actions are available, but the trigger is a forecast rather than a live
observed bottleneck, and the capacity is provisioned across edge hosts
and rented cloud instances, with the outcome measured in tenancy cost. 

    Even among the most recent surveys regarding autoscaling, 
El Mrabet et al. extend the classification across cloud,
fog, and edge, categorising the surveyed methods by their
decision mechanism, scaling strategy, and
deployment layer, and the provisioned tier is
not among the classification dimensions
\parencite{ElMrabet2025ComprehensiveSurveyTaxonomyAutoscalingTechniques}.

Qu et al. describe a web application as split into
a front-end, application logic, and a database tier, and
record that \enquote{the database tier is often considered dynamically
unscalable and ignored by auto-scalers}
\parencite[p.~6]{Qu2018AutoScalingWebApplicationsClouds}.

    Overall, the literature demonstrates that the kind of capacity added
under certain scenarios can have an impact,
compute replicas do not relieve pressure whose limiting
resource lies in the storage tier, and database capacity can govern SLA
violations while a compute-tier scaling decision remains unchanged.
Scaling actions are therefore not interchangeable merely because each
adds resources.

    Therefore in the reviewed studies, the choice between these actions
remains an architectural given, settled by forecasts or by joint
placement rather than selected at runtime from a live observed
bottleneck. This dissertation isolates that selection, with storage
scale-up among the actions the controller may select, and measures
the resulting differences in targeted-tier relief, service recovery,
and resource use. How that decision is handed to the infrastructure is examined,
with the other interfaces, in Section~\ref{sec:lit_synthesis}.

\section{Load Balancing and Readiness Admission on SDN}\label{sec:lit_sdn_lb} %DONE
Load balancing distributes incoming traffic among a set of available
backends so that no single backend is overloaded while others remain
underutilised. In SDN networks this distribution is programmed centrally,
where the controller installs forwarding rules that steer each flow to a
selected backend, and a selection policy decides which backend
receives the next flow, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:sdn_load_balancing}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/sdn_load_balancing_v3.png}
    \caption{Load balancing in an SDN network: the controller installs
    forwarding rules that distribute client traffic among the available
    backends.}
    \label{fig:sdn_load_balancing}
\end{figure}

    Load balancing is a common application of SDN. At a given routing
decision, a load-balancing policy operates over the set of backends
currently eligible to receive traffic, and the reviewed studies
concentrate on optimising the distribution of load over that set, using
criteria such as load, path properties, and resource utilisation
\parencite{Neghabi2018LoadBalancingMechanismsSoftwareDefined,Belgaum2020SystematicReviewLoadBalancingTechniques}.

    Kurma, a load balancer for geo-distributed storage systems, is
explicitly designed to operate at a granularity of seconds so that it
can \enquote{work in tandem with modern elastic controllers, thereby
reducing over-provisioning and SLO violations incurred during
provisioning delays} \parencite[p.~386]{Bogdanov2018Kurma}. The delay between
provisioning and usable capacity is therefore a recognised cost, yet
the interval in which readiness state propagates to the routing plane
is not separated from the surrounding lifecycle.

    Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. combine monitoring, auto-scaling, and load
balancing in an SDN redirector for a transparent VNF cluster, so that
load balancing operates over a pool of instances that is resized while
traffic is being served. Their system changes the number of active
cluster members and removes flow rules for unhealthy instances using
status obtained through the MANO monitoring path
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing}.
It therefore demonstrates that dynamic membership is already a legitimate
concern. Its evaluation, however, reports activation at the granularity of
the integrated system, so the interval from verified application readiness
to routing eligibility is not separated from the surrounding lifecycle.

    The membership of the eligible set, however, is not fixed. Backends are
added as demand rises and removed as it recedes, and a backend becomes
usable capacity only once it can serve the application, its readiness
state has propagated to the routing plane, and it is included in the
eligible set. The interval between verified readiness and routing eligibility is
therefore an admission delay, and it lies outside the scope of the
selection studies reviewed here, which, as far as admission is
concerned, take the eligible set as given. Indeed, Neghabi et al.
record failure management as a concern that the mechanisms their
survey covers have not taken into account, so failure-driven changes
to the eligible set remain unaddressed
\parencite{Neghabi2018LoadBalancingMechanismsSoftwareDefined}. 
    Outside those studies, managing the pool itself is an established
problem. Dreibholz and
Mazumdar apply the IETF Reliable Server Pooling framework to a pool
spanning edge and multi-cloud resources. A member registers to the pool
when it joins and de-registers when it leaves, while a registrar keeps
its availability current through periodic keep-alive checks. The
framework's own analysis notes that \enquote{propagating load updates
takes time, leading to temporary inaccuracy}, and the mitigation for
that inaccuracy is left to the selection layer, in policies that
discount stale pool state
\parencite[p.~6]{Dreibholz2023TowardsLightweightTaskSchedulingFramework}.

The eligible set and the true serving state of its backends can therefore
desynchronise in one of two directions. When a change reaches the
routing plane late or not at all, ready capacity stays outside the set,
and the fleet serves the demand without it. When a backend is in the
set while it cannot serve the traffic it receives, the requests routed
to it fail.

    The reviewed selection studies optimise the distribution of load over the
eligible set, taking that set as given. This dissertation treats the set
itself as the variable: it studies how the arrival of newly provisioned,
ready capacity into the eligible set affects when and whether that
capacity carries load, the admission interval from verified readiness to
routing eligibility, the time until the new capacity serves traffic, and
how quickly that membership change reaches the load distribution and the
existing fleet. Direct lifecycle notification and periodic discovery are
the two operational mechanisms of that coordination interface, with the
bounded fallback variant and the wake-and-verify variant evaluated in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{SDN-Based Orchestration Platforms}\label{sec:lit_sdn_orchestration} %DONE
Resource orchestration is the management layer that keeps a running
service within its requirements by coordinating the compute, network,
and storage resources of the infrastructure on which it runs. It is
the activity that the technologies and tools reviewed in
Section~\ref{sec:platform_background} are assembled to serve. Edge sites
supply the resources, SDN supplies the programmable connectivity,
containers supply the unit of compute, and the document store holds
the service state. That decision-making, and its execution, is
orchestration, and at the edge it is all the more demanding because each
site holds scarce capacity and the demand on it fluctuates over time.
For stateful services, the scaling action can address either side,
because compute replicas serve requests, while the data
they serve lives in the storage tier.
Demand for data can therefore exist without demand for compute and vice 
versa.

    This dissertation studies the SDN-based form of this architectural
layer, in which the coordination spans the SDN controller, the
container runtime, and the data store, and the challenge is to keep
these layers working together as demand fluctuates. The
platforms reviewed here bind these layers with different degrees of
coupling, and this section examines how closely the three functions
of the demand-to-capacity chain, monitoring, scaling, and routing,
are held together in each, using the terms interface and coordination
gap as defined in Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}.

    Baktir et al. organise the gains SDN brings to
edge computing into benefit areas such as high-resolution control
built on a centralised view and programmatic access
\parencite{Baktir2017HowCanEdgeComputingBenefit}. Wang et al. survey
architectures in which SDN and NFV jointly coordinate placement,
traffic, and caching across the network and service layers, showing
that SDN's role at the edge extends beyond forwarding into
orchestration
\parencite{Wang2019SoftwareDefinedNetworkingEnhancedEdge}. Sofia
et al. argue for context-aware cross-layer orchestration of
containerised edge-cloud applications serving next-generation IoT
services, treating contextual information such as location and data
freshness as part of the orchestration input
\parencite{Sofia2023DynamicContextAwareCrossLayer}. These works
document architectures or proofs of concept rather than the
coordination inside a running system, and the interfaces between
the functions fall outside the scope of all three.


    Peng et al. exercise SDN-based resource management in vehicular
multi-access edge computing as task migration and radio slicing,
treating telemetry as available input rather than as an interface to
be studied, and without container orchestration or tier scaling
\parencite{Peng2019SDNResourceManagementAutonomousVehicular}.

    Some of these platforms bring the functions
together at the SDN control point itself. Llorens-Carrodeguas et al.
combine monitoring, auto-scaling, and load balancing in a single SDN
redirector for a transparent VNF cluster, although metric collection
and the lifecycle operations that feed and execute the decision
remain external
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing};
Hung et al. present an SDN controller for 5G backhaul that closes a
telemetry-and-routing loop, with telemetry collection delegated to
decentralised microservices reached through an API, omitting scaling
altogether
\parencite{Hung2022SDNControllerMonitoringArchitecture5G}. The first
study binds all three functions at one control point and the
second binds two of the three, so combining the functions at an SDN
control point has precedent in the literature
and is leveraged in this dissertation. Neither system, however, reports the
delay within its coordination.

    At the geo-distributed scale, three systems coordinate the same
functions. OneEdge keeps authoritative state at each edge site,
reconciles it with a central aggregate through two-phase commits, and
uses \ac{ZMQ} for communication among its distributed components. Its
evaluation reports end-to-end deployment latency with a coarse
breakdown into scheduling delay, WAN overhead, and container creation,
but not the cost of each control-plane interaction
\parencite{Saurez2021OneEdge}. McK8s deploys four controllers on a
management cluster as Kubernetes operators over custom resources,
connects member clusters through a Cilium mesh, and measures
inter-cluster latencies with Serf, treating those latencies as input
to placement rather than as an effect to study
\parencite{Tamiru2021Mck8sOrchestrationPlatformGeoDistributed}.
Kurma instances exchange load, latency, and SLO-violation
measurements among datacenters and redirect requests at second
granularity while elastic controllers provision capacity, naming the
stages of the provisioning delay, detection, acquisition, and
warm-up, before treating them as one combined quantity
\parencite{Bogdanov2018Kurma}. The coordination is built into each system, 
but the delay it carries is not isolated. 
None of their evaluations attributes the time taken by the state
updates, exchanges, and redirects along those communication paths to
the individual handoffs between observing demand, deciding, provisioning,
and admitting capacity.

    The related background is therefore organised in two modes. In the dominant
mode, monitoring, scaling, and routing are implemented as separate
components, each running its own control loop, so the evidence and
commands that tie them together must cross component boundaries.
The separation is not only architectural. In an evaluation across major
cloud providers, Podolskiy et al. observe IaaS autoscalers and the
surrounding layers acting independently, with load-balancing issues and
increased latency appearing around demand shifts
\parencite{Podolskiy2018IaaSReactiveAutoscalingPerformanceChallenges}. The
measurement is observational, documenting the coordination symptom in
cloud deployments rather than isolating an interface.
In a second, recurring mode, the same functions are kept together at an
SDN-based control point or coordinated through shared state. What
neither mode provides, however, is a decomposition of the control
process into its interfaces. Prior systems measure complete control
processes or coordinate them by construction.
They do not isolate telemetry delivery, scaling-action selection, or
readiness admission as independent variables in the demand-to-capacity path.
That interface-level isolation is what this dissertation provides.

    SDN-based resource orchestration is therefore an established and
feasible arrangement, and the coordination it rests on is a recorded
open issue. Nain et al. record resource optimisation in SDN-based
edge environments among the continuing challenges of the field
\parencite{Nain2024ResourceOptimizationEdgeSDNEdge}. The interface
properties studied in this dissertation are one measurable dimension of
that coordination, and because the functions meet at boundaries that
an SDN-based control point can expose as explicit, configurable
interfaces, such a platform is a suitable experimental basis for
evaluating them. The platform built in
this dissertation follows that arrangement and exposes telemetry
delivery, scaling-action selection, and readiness admission as
independently configurable interfaces, as presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:system_architecture}.

\section{Synthesis and Research Gaps}\label{sec:lit_synthesis} % DONE
    The review has examined the functions of resource
orchestration. Where the reviewed works address the cost of these
crossings, it is treated at three levels. That cost is first
acknowledged as real. Kurma's
seconds-granularity operation aims to reduce the over-provisioning
and SLO violations incurred during provisioning delays
\parencite{Bogdanov2018Kurma}, and Yaseen records the visibility gaps
that pull-based polling leaves and calls for monitoring tightly
integrated with control
\parencite{Yaseen2025CountersTelemetrySurveyProgrammableNetwork}.
Second, where the cost is evaluated, it is evaluated only in
composite. Llorens-Carrodeguas et al. report activation at the
granularity of the integrated system, OneEdge reports end-to-end
deployment latency breaking it down into scheduling, network, and
container-creation components, and Kurma presents the provisioning
delay as one combined quantity, none attributing the measured time
to an individual handoff delay
\parencite{Llorens2021SDNBasedHorizontalAutoScalingLoadBalancing,
Saurez2021OneEdge,Bogdanov2018Kurma}.
Third, the cost is never materialised as an experimental variable in a
stateful service. The interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain are
acknowledged and, in places, combined, but no reviewed work traces the
effect of an individual interface through such a service. The closest reviewed
precedent is AdapPF, which varies
monitoring freshness and measures a downstream consequence, but only
for a single evaluated consumer, a scheduler
\parencite{Huang2023AdapPFSelfAdaptiveScrapeInterval}. The separation
that produces these delays is not an artefact of one platform family
but the shared default of the areas reviewed. 
What varies across them
is only how far each acknowledges the boundaries it imposes. The
placement literature, as surveyed by Tabatabaee Malazi et al., for
instance, names the interval between an instance's arrival and its
readiness to serve as launch time, but the step from readiness to
traffic admission lies outside its scope
\parencite{TabatabaeeMalazi2022DynamicServicePlacementMultiAccess}.

    No reviewed work materialises the other properties of these crossings as
experimental variables either. The loss of intermediate evidence is
recorded as a limitation of polling
(Section~\ref{sec:lit_monitoring}), the tier that a capacity action
serves is settled by forecasts or by joint placement rather than selected
from a live observed bottleneck (Section~\ref{sec:lit_auto_scaling}), and
failure-driven changes to the eligible set remain unaddressed by the 
reviewed mechanisms (Section~\ref{sec:lit_sdn_lb}).

    Table~\ref{tab:interface_gaps} condenses these findings into the
three interfaces and the question each leaves open.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{The three interfaces of the demand-to-capacity chain and
    the questions they leave open.}
    \label{tab:interface_gaps}
    \begin{tabular}{p{0.34\textwidth} p{0.58\textwidth}}
        \toprule
        Interface & Open question \\
        \midrule
        Telemetry delivery to decision (RQ1) &
        Does a controller behave differently when demand evidence
        arrives late but complete, versus when intermediate
        observations are absent? \\
        Decision to scaling action (RQ2) &
        When compute and storage actions are both available, how much does
        selecting the action matched to an observed compute-bound or
        data-access-bound bottleneck change targeted-tier relief, service
        recovery, and resource use relative to fixed single-tier
        policies, and where do the effects concentrate? \\
        Ready backend to traffic (RQ3) &
        How does readiness-admission coordination affect the delay from
        verified readiness to usable capacity, when the new capacity begins
        to carry load, the onset-window service consequence, and what the
        admission evidence guarantees when the readiness claim is lost or
        premature? \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

    The research questions of Section~\ref{sec:research_questions} are the
experimental form of Table~\ref{tab:interface_gaps}. This
dissertation does not argue that the coordination gap is universal,
only that, within the reviewed literature, the interfaces that
compose it have not been isolated as independent variables.

    The evaluation follows the same per-interface discipline, and the
demand-to-capacity chain is treated as a sequence of separately
measured interfaces, whose aggregate cost this dissertation
deliberately does not claim.

    Read together, the reviewed works provide evidence of the topics
studied in this thesis: the delivery of demand evidence (its freshness and
completeness) and its effect on the link between monitoring and decision, 
the consequences of serving the wrong tier when the bottleneck lies elsewhere,
and the interval between provisioning and usable capacity. They also show that
coordinating these functions at an SDN control point is an established
approach.

    The questions also sit between the surveyed fields rather than
inside any of them. Each area's abstraction serves its own question.
Chapter~\ref{ch:system_architecture} presents the platform designed
to make them askable and measurable.


\chapter{Architecture and Design of the Proposed Solution for Edge Services}\label{ch:system_architecture}
This chapter presents the architecture and design of the proposed
cross-layer \ac{SDN} orchestration platform for stateful edge services.
The design provides a platform that runs controller-side telemetry
consumption, elasticity coordination, and traffic steering in one controller
process per domain. The delivery of aggregated telemetry, the selection of the scaling
action, and the lifecycle transitions from scaling action to traffic admission
remain explicit observation points. 

    Section~\ref{sec:design_requirements} defines the requirements that guided
the proposed solution, and Section~\ref{sec:architecture_overview} presents
the two-domain architecture and its communication paths
(Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_overview}).
Section~\ref{sec:elastic_allocation} describes the distributed elastic
allocation of compute and storage resources, Section~\ref{sec:monitoring_decision}
the monitoring and decision engine, and Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow}
the control workflow. Together, these sections realise the design
requirements: cross-layer coordination (DR1) and fast-path isolation (DR5)
through the single-process architecture and its execution contexts,
independent compute and storage elasticity (DR3) and lifecycle-safe
admission (DR4) through the elastic allocation, separability of the control
interfaces (DR2) through the configurable delivery, selection, and admission
designs, and end-to-end observability (DR6) through the instrumented chain.

\section{Design Requirements}\label{sec:design_requirements}
The platform was designed to support stateful edge services whose demand and
resource requirements vary over time. The requirements below follow from the
coordination problem and the research gaps established in
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review}, and guide the design
presented in the remainder of the chapter.

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textit{DR\arabic*:}, leftmargin=*]
    \item \textit{Cross-layer coordination.} Within each domain, telemetry
    consumption, topology awareness, resource lifecycle management, and
    traffic steering must share controller state within the same process.
    Aggregated telemetry must reach the controller through a structured,
    configurable delivery interface. Peer controllers must share the topology
    state needed for cross-domain awareness.

    \item \textit{Separability of control interfaces.} The delivery of demand
    evidence, the selection of a scaling action, and the admission of ready
    capacity to traffic must remain independently configurable.

    \item \textit{Independent compute and storage elasticity.} The platform
    must distinguish an increase or decrease in compute capacity from an
    increase or decrease in storage capacity, allowing each resource tier to
    be managed separately.

    \item \textit{Lifecycle-safe admission.} Provisioned resources must not be
    regarded as usable capacity before they are admitted to the appropriate
    routing path, and where a readiness signal is configured, it must be
    satisfied before admission.

    \item \textit{Fast-path isolation.} Packet handling must remain separate
    from slow infrastructure operations, such as container creation, replica
    preparation, and resource removal.

    \item \textit{End-to-end observability.} The platform must record the path
    from demand observation to the first successful request served by newly
    available capacity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Two-Domain Organisation}

The platform is organised as two network domains connected by an emulated
inter-domain data link, representing the two sites of the evaluated
deployment. Each domain contains client workloads, an \ac{OVS} data plane,
a pool of containerised edge servers, a MongoDB replica-set storage tier, a
telemetry aggregator, and an \ac{SDN} controller. The peer
controllers exchange topology information, and each consumes the telemetry
summaries published by the aggregators of both domains. Each controller runs
telemetry consumption, elasticity coordination, and traffic steering in the
same process for its own domain.

    The emulated inter-domain link models the cross-domain data path, while
telemetry delivery and controller-to-controller topology exchange use
host-local control channels. This distinction separates data-path delay from
control-path communication.

\subsection{Virtual IP Data Paths}\label{sec:vip_data_paths}
Clients access edge services through a per-domain \ac{VIP}. The virtual service
addresses are \texttt{VIP\_SERVER\_N1} and \texttt{VIP\_SERVER\_N2}, collectively
referred to as \texttt{VIP\_SERVER}. Edge servers route storage reads through
the virtual data addresses \texttt{VIP\_DATA\_N1} and \texttt{VIP\_DATA\_N2},
collectively referred to as \texttt{VIP\_DATA}, and issue storage writes
directly to the replica-set primary. This separation represents service routing
and data access as distinct routing surfaces.

    When a flow addressed to a virtual IP is first observed, the controller
selects an eligible backend and configures the \ac{OVS} data plane to forward
the flow. Subsequent matching packets are handled in the data plane without further
controller involvement. Service backend selection may include eligible backends
in either domain (a platform capability held constant in the evaluation), while 
cross-domain storage placement is disabled.

    Backend and storage-member selection are cost-based. The controller
computes a \ac{WSM} cost from each eligible backend's cached resource
metrics (CPU utilisation, memory usage, request count, and hop distance) and
installs forwarding rules for the lowest-cost one. 
Each metric enters the cost as a fraction of the largest value observed
among the eligible candidates, so metrics with different units contribute
on a common scale under configurable weights (reported per campaign in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}), and candidates that tie at the lowest
cost are selected in round-robin. Hop distance expresses reach and in this 
architecture a peer-domain candidate is always assigned more hops than a local
one, so local candidates are preferred at comparable resource state.
For storage reads observed on the virtual data path, the same mechanism
selects a replica-set member, using the corresponding storage metrics (CPU
utilisation, memory usage, connections, replication lag, and hop distance).
Figure~\ref{fig:vip_routing_sequence} walks through the resulting sequence.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/vip_routing_sequence_v2.png}
    \caption{Double-VIP routing sequence: a client request addressed to
    \texttt{VIP\_SERVER} triggers backend selection in the controller, and
    the edge server's storage reads through \texttt{VIP\_DATA} trigger the
    selection of a replica-set member.}
    \label{fig:vip_routing_sequence}
\end{figure}

The selection policy also defines two behaviours that accompany the cost
function: a warm-lease priority, which gives a newly admitted backend
bounded priority for new connections during a configured window, and a
slow-start ramp, in which a newly discovered backend is held back by a
cost penalty that decays over the same window. Both are configuration
choices of the routing path, held constant across the compared arms of
the reported campaigns.

A request's path decomposes into two intervals. The first is the
\emph{pre-dispatch wait}: the time between the client issuing the request and
the request's dispatch in the serving process, covering the setup of its
connection through the routing path and any wait ahead of the
serving process. The second is the
\emph{edge service time}: the interval from request dispatch to response
finalisation in the serving process, which itself decomposes into a
\emph{data-access} component (the MongoDB operations issued for the request,
including connection acquisition, when applicable by the edge server) and
an \emph{edge-processing} component (scheduling, lease and cache work, and
response serialisation). Their sum is the request's \emph{user-visible latency},
the client-side round trip. The two edge-service components are the tier-specific
latency signals the scaling policy observes, data access for the storage tier and
processing for the compute tier (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}). The
pre-dispatch wait is observable only as a difference and carries no separate
instrumentation.

Edge servers and storage components emit resource and request observations to
their local telemetry aggregator through \ac{ZMQ}, and each aggregator forms
windowed summaries for the controller. These summaries inform both backend
selection and elasticity decisions, while topology exchange
maintains awareness of peer-domain resources.
The aggregation-to-controller link is the configurable telemetry-delivery
interface, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:telemetry_delivery_paths}.

    The evaluated deployment runs the two domains on a single host, and only

The two dimensions are summarised in Figure~\ref{fig:elastic_allocation} and

\subsection{Scaling Policy}\label{sec:scaling_policy}
The policy is applied per resource tier, with tier-specific
signals and parameters. Each tier is observed through two signals: a
utilisation signal, which in this platform is the CPU utilisation of the
tier's units, and a latency signal. For each telemetry window $w$, let
$c(w)$ denote the window's utilisation and $t(w)$ its typical latency
(the tier's window latency statistic, the mean or the median as configured
per campaign). Each signal is first mapped onto the unit
interval through two calibration parameters, a floor and a span. A value at
or below its floor contributes nothing, a value at or above the floor plus
its span contributes the maximum, and intermediate values contribute
proportionally. The floor separates normal operation from pressure, and the
span sets how far the signal  must rise before its contribution saturates.
Because both signals are mapped onto this bounded scale, they contribute on
common terms despite different units, and a single extreme
window cannot dominate the score. The two normalised
components are combined with weights $w_{\mathrm{util}}$ and
$w_{\mathrm{lat}}$ into the degradation score
\begin{equation}\label{eq:degradation_score}
\begin{split}
    s(w) &= w_{\mathrm{util}}\,\hat{c}(w) + w_{\mathrm{lat}}\,\hat{t}(w), \\
    \hat{c}(w) &= \min\left(1,\ \max\left(0,\ \frac{c(w)-c_0}{c_\Delta}\right)\right), \\
    \hat{t}(w) &= \min\left(1,\ \max\left(0,\ \frac{t(w)-t_0}{t_\Delta}\right)\right),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
with the per-tier weights configured to sum to one, so the score lies
between zero and one, where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
    \item $c(w)$ -- CPU utilisation of the scored tier observed in window $w$;
    \item $t(w)$ -- typical latency of the scored tier observed in window $w$;
    \item $c_0$, $t_0$ -- signal floors: at or below these values a signal is considered normal for the tier;
    \item $c_\Delta$, $t_\Delta$ -- signal spans: the rise above the floor at which a signal's contribution saturates;
    \item $\hat{c}(w)$, $\hat{t}(w)$ -- normalised components of window $w$, each in $[0,1]$;
    \item $w_{\mathrm{util}}$, $w_{\mathrm{lat}}$ -- configurable weights of the utilisation and latency components.
\end{itemize}
    The floors, spans, weights, and threshold parameters are configured
independently for each tier, so the policy can be calibrated to each tier
without changing the structure of the score. For the compute tier the signals are CPU
utilisation and request-processing latency, while for the storage tier
they are storage-member CPU utilisation and data-access latency. These
signals feed only the scaling decisions, while backend and storage-member
selection uses the cached resource metrics described in
Section~\ref{sec:architecture_overview}.

    A window is degraded when its score reaches an adaptive
threshold, and a scale-up requires sustained degradation across recent windows. 
Let $\mathcal{W}_n$ be the set of the last $W$ windows in which the tier was 
eligible to act, and $R$ the required number of degraded windows; the scale-up 
condition is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:scale_up_condition}
\begin{split}
    \left|\,\{\, w \in \mathcal{W}_n : s(w) \geq \theta(k) \,\}\right| &\geq R, \\
    \theta(k) &= \min\big(\theta_{\mathrm{base}} + k\,\Delta\theta + \beta_{\mathrm{peer}},\ \theta_{\max}\big),
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
    \item $\mathcal{W}_n$ -- sliding set of the most recent $W$ windows in which the tier was eligible to act;
    \item $W$ -- number of eligible windows in the scale-up window;
    \item $R$ -- minimum number of degraded windows required to request a scale-up;
    \item $k$ -- number of dynamic units currently in the tier's pool (units provisioned by the controller beyond the initial pool);
    \item $\theta(k)$ -- adaptive degradation threshold with $k$ dynamic units in the pool;
    \item $\theta_{\mathrm{base}}$ -- base threshold;
    \item $\Delta\theta$ -- threshold increment per additional dynamic unit, applied linearly for the compute tier; for the storage tier the increment diminishes with each additional unit, down to a configured minimum;
    \item $\theta_{\max}$ -- upper bound of the adaptive threshold;
    \item $\beta_{\mathrm{peer}}$ -- peer relief added to the compute-tier threshold while the peer domain is healthy enough to absorb spillover traffic (zero otherwise).
\end{itemize}
    The threshold follows the number of dynamic units in the tier's pool.
Each additional unit therefore faces a higher bar, and the threshold falls
back as units are removed. A scale-up cooldown after each committed scale-up
and a scale-down cooldown keyed on the most recent scale-up keep the pace of
actions bounded, together with a per-tier cap on the number of units. The
window advances only when the tier was eligible to act (not blocked by an
ongoing infrastructure operation, not at its cap, and not within a scale-up
cooldown), and it is cleared when a scale-up occurs. A committed scale-up also
clears the tier's scale-down window, so a recent scale-up is not immediately
reversed.

    A window is declared idle when both signals fall below their idle
thresholds,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:idle_condition}
    c(w) < \tau_c \quad\text{and}\quad t(w) < \tau_t,
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
    \item $\tau_c$, $\tau_t$ -- idle thresholds of the utilisation and latency signals.
\end{itemize}
    A scale-down is requested only when idleness is sustained across
$R_\downarrow$ of the last $W_\downarrow$ windows, where $R_\downarrow$ is the
required number of idle windows and $W_\downarrow$ the length of the
scale-down window. This window is longer than the scale-up one, which
prevents a transient data-bound stall from being misread as idle capacity. 
Windows whose latency exceeds a ceiling are
treated as indeterminate and do not count toward idleness.

    The policy evaluates each tier per telemetry window, in a fixed order:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=0pt]
    \item the window is skipped when the tier is ineligible: an
    infrastructure operation is in progress, the tier is at its cap, or a
    scale-up cooldown is active;
    \item the degradation score $s(w)$ is computed from the two normalised
    signals~\eqref{eq:degradation_score};
    \item if at least $R$ of the last $W$ eligible windows reach the adaptive
    threshold $\theta(k)$~\eqref{eq:scale_up_condition}, the tier requests
    a scale-up and the window history is cleared;
    \item otherwise, if both signals fall below their idle
    thresholds~\eqref{eq:idle_condition}, idleness is tracked, and the
    tier requests a scale-down once idleness spans $R_\downarrow$ of the
    last $W_\downarrow$ windows.
\end{enumerate}
    The parameter values used in the
evaluation are reported in Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup} and listed
per campaign in Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}.

\subsection{Compute}\label{sec:elastic_compute}
Compute capacity is a per-domain pool of containerised edge servers, each
capable of serving any request addressed to the domain's virtual service
address. Storage reads performed by a compute replica are routed through the
domain's virtual data address, or served from a Tier~1 copy where one is
held (the data-placement tiers are defined in Section~\ref{sec:elastic_data}),
and its writes are issued directly to the replica-set primary. 
Reads are left to the routing path. Compute scale-up
applies the policy defined in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}.

    A newly provisioned compute replica is recorded through an explicit
registration step and becomes eligible for backend selection only
once it is visible in the routing pool. How readiness
reaches that pool is configurable: it may be propagated by a direct
lifecycle notification from the replica, or discovered by the controller
through periodic probes, and the design may additionally bound the damage
of a lost notification. A fallback probe re-derives readiness after a
configured window, between the pure event-coupled and pure polling styles.
The notification may also serve only to wake an immediate probe, so that
it opens the check but does not admit on its own. Because the direct
rule admits on the claim itself, a claim that arrives before the replica
can serve would reach the routing pool unchecked, while the probe-based
rules re-derive readiness before admitting.
Because the backend selector steers new flows while installed flows remain
where they were placed (Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow}), admission also
marks the point at which the new backend can begin to share the fleet's load.

    Per-backend resource quotas are configuration parameters of the compute
tier. The fault contrast uses a deliberately reduced per-backend CPU quota, so
that the existing fleet carries thin spare capacity, the condition under
which a lost admission, if consequential, is exposed rather than absorbed
by slack. The evaluated values are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}.

    Compute scale-down removes the least disruptive eligible replica, applying
the idle condition defined in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}. 
Only replicas provisioned by the platform are eligible for removal, so the
fleet's initial replicas are left undisturbed. A candidate is considered
only while its reports are recent, and among the eligible replicas the one
carrying the least request load is removed first, with the lowest
utilisation and request-processing latency used as tie-breakers.
Removal proceeds in two phases: the replica is first withdrawn
from the routing pool and signalled to finish its in-flight requests,
and only after it drains is its network attachment removed.
A pending drain can be cancelled if load rebounds before the replica exits,
favouring fast recovery over aggressive reclamation.

\subsection{Storage}\label{sec:elastic_data}
The replica set is the unit in which storage capacity is scaled. The storage
tier of each domain runs as a MongoDB replica set whose primary serves the
clients' writes and whose secondaries replicate the data and absorb the
remainder of the traffic. Reads may therefore be served by any replica-set
member, and the consistency contract is primary writes with eventually
consistent secondary reads. The evaluation does not rely on read-after-write
semantics. Storage scale-up is a membership change. The controller provisions
a new storage member, which joins the set as a non-voting secondary, and
admits it to the data path only once the member can serve reads. In the 
evaluated configuration, preparation is decoupled from the reaction: a readied 
member is held in reserve outside the routing pool, and the storage scale-up is
the activation of this prepared reserve, which removes the initial readiness wait
from the reaction path. A new reserve is prepared immediately afterwards.

    Storage scale-down removes an idle member while the remaining members keep
serving. Only members added by the platform are eligible for removal, so the
set's original membership is left undisturbed, and the most recently added
eligible member is removed first. The decision applies the idle condition
defined in Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy} to the storage tier's idle signals
(its data-access latency and, in the configurations that enable
it, its storage utilisation).

    Beyond the membership change, the platform defines three data-placement
tiers: Tier~0 provides direct remote reads over
the virtual data path, meaning the replica-set member is deployed outside its
primary network domain, Tier~1 holds a partial copy of the data in the consumer
domain, and Tier~2 is the replica-set extension described above. Tier~0 and
Tier~1 are platform capabilities rather than evaluated treatments. In the
experiments, the storage action is limited to activating the prepared
reserve or removing a member of the domain's own replica set.

\section{Monitoring and Decision Engine}\label{sec:monitoring_decision}
Observation begins at the producers. Each compute replica emits a
per-request record of its resource state and request experience, and each
storage member's companion process emits replication and resource-state
samples as the member reports activity, with an optional periodic heartbeat.
A per-domain aggregator collects these records and publishes a windowed
summary at a fixed cadence, so that time is observed as a regular sequence
of windows and every window, including idle ones, is evaluated and recorded.
The summary contains the utilisation and latency signals
that the scaling policy consumes, together with the per-backend metrics used
by routing.

    Observation also carries liveness. A node that is idle may keep reporting
through a periodic heartbeat, so a quiet but alive node remains present in
the summaries, and each summary carries the time of each node's last report.
The controller bounds its knowledge by that recency: a node that stays silent
beyond an absence bound is treated as gone and cleaned up, so the platform
does not act on capacity it cannot observe.

    The delivery of these summaries to the controller is an explicit, configurable
interface. The default delivery pushes each completed window as it is
published. Alternative delivery modes supply the same windowed evidence in
other forms, such as periodic retrieval of only the most recent window
(latest-state delivery), periodic sampling of the published stream, delivering
every Nth completed window as it is published and dropping the
intermediate windows (sampled-push delivery), or ordered replay
of a retained window history (event-preserving delivery, immediate or
delayed without burst replay), so that the effect of delayed or incomplete demand
evidence can be studied while the aggregation, the workload, and the decision logic
remain unchanged.

    Inside the controller, the summaries feed two consumers. The first is the
routing state. Per-backend metrics update the state that backend selection
uses. The second is the decision engine, which evaluates each window with the
scaling policy of Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy} and emits a typed,
prioritised action request once a tier's condition is sustained. Requests that
add or preserve capacity take precedence over requests that remove capacity.
Among the additions, a storage scale-up precedes a compute scale-up, and among
the removals, a storage scale-down precedes a compute scale-down. A request's
type identifies its resource tier (compute or storage) and
its action (scale-up or scale-down), and the evidence behind each request
is recorded in the decision log for the window that produced it.
Figure~\ref{fig:decision_engine_flow} details the
per-window evaluation, from the eligibility check to the request emissions.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/decision_engine_flow_v2.png}
    \caption{The per-window evaluation, in the fixed order of
    Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}: (1) the eligibility check, where the
    window is skipped when the tier is ineligible; (2) the degradation score
    $s(w)$, computed from the normalised signals; (3) the scale-up condition:
    a scale-up request is emitted and the window history is cleared when at
    least $R$ of the last $W$ eligible windows reach the adaptive threshold
    $\theta(k)$; and (4) the idle condition, tested otherwise: idleness is
    tracked, excluding indeterminate windows, and a scale-down request is

    Every remaining branch returns to the next telemetry window.}
    \label{fig:decision_engine_flow}

    The decision engine may also be configured to commit to a single action per
window when several tiers signal simultaneously, which is the selection
interface of the demand-to-capacity chain. In the bottleneck-aware
configuration, that selection distinguishes between compute-bound and
data-access-bound demand and scales the matching tier when it leads by the
configured classification margin (Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}),
resolving close scores towards storage. A per-tier action budget can bound its actions,
and the selected and rejected actions are recorded as evidence. Lifecycle 
signals, such as the completion of a drain or the readiness of a storage member,
reach the same decision path as control events, so that admission and release
follow the same flow as the actions themselves.

\section{Control Workflow}\label{sec:control_workflow}
The control workflow separates three execution contexts that share one
process but do not block one another. The packet context handles flow
decisions and does not wait on slow work. The observation context evaluates
telemetry windows and emits action requests. The action context performs
infrastructure changes such as provisioning, drain, and removal.

    The workflow traverses the demand-to-capacity chain introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:context_motivation}. A change in demand alters the request
experience reported by the compute replicas, and the window that contains
the change is published and delivered. The decision engine evaluates the
delivered window. If the tier condition is sustained, the engine emits an
action request, which the action context executes. The provisioned unit
reaches its readiness state and is admitted to the corresponding routing
pool, which for the storage tier is the activation of the prepared reserve.
New flows can then be directed to it, while flows already installed on other
members remain where they were placed. The chain completes when the new unit
serves its first successful request. Each step is instrumented, so the
timeline from demand observation to usable capacity is recorded end to end,
from registration and routing-pool inclusion to the first successful request
(Figure~\ref{fig:control_workflow}). 
When readiness admission is enabled, a
compute replica that does not reach readiness within the configured bound, or
that is never admitted within the abandonment bound, is abandoned and torn
down instead of being admitted. Without readiness admission,
compute replicas are registered as soon as they are provisioned and no
readiness bound applies, so a replica that never becomes usable is reclaimed
only by the ordinary lifecycle checks.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/control_workflow_v5.png}
    \caption{The control workflow, drawn as a UML diagram. The
    demand-to-capacity chain runs across three contexts in the same process:
    the packet context, the observation context, and the action context. The

    structures (the telemetry state, the action queue, the lifecycle state, and

    The numbered stages match the chain in

    \label{fig:control_workflow}
\end{figure}

    State crosses these contexts mostly through shared structures rather than
through direct calls. The observation context writes the telemetry state
and the action requests, and the action context owns the infrastructure
changes and records the lifecycle state of each in-flight operation. The
routing pools are updated with the lifecycle transitions: a unit enters them
when it is admitted and leaves them on removal, while a readied member held
in reserve remains outside them until activation. The packet context reads 
both on each flow decision. Because the packet context is not blocked by
the action context, a long-running change cannot stall request steering.
Because the action requests are queued and prioritised, detection is not
blocked by execution. 

    Every request that adds or preserves capacity
precedes every request that removes capacity. The cancellation of a
pending drain precedes its execution. 
This execution order is distinct from
the action selection, which the observation context decides before the
request is queued.

    Across domains, the peer controllers exchange topology state so that each
controller is aware of the peer domain's resources, while acting on its own
domain's demand. The concrete realisation of this design, the technologies
that implement each context, and the validation of the resulting platform
are the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation}. The experimental
characterisation of the three interfaces is presented in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Experimental Infrastructure}\label{sec:impl_infrastructure}

The platform runs on a single cloud VM host that holds both domains as

The two domains are joined by a router container whose inter-domain link
carries an emulated, fixed symmetric round-trip time applied with tc-netem
(185~ms in the reported campaigns, with the jitter, loss, and rate knobs
available and off by default). The evaluated data path is single-site. The
emulated link carries no evaluated traffic, and every control-plane link
is host-local.

Each domain has two virtual service addresses, one for client requests
and one for storage reads. The data plane combines OpenFlow rules with
the kernel's connection tracking. On the service path, the controller
installs a DNAT/SNAT rule pair for the client and the selected backend.
With flow isolation enabled the pair is scoped to a single connection and
removed after its response, so every measured request triggers a fresh
selection. On the data path, a forward rule selects the member and
commits a NAT mapping to it, and the kernel records the connection by its
five-tuple, the pair of addresses and ports plus the protocol. Reply
packets are handed back to connection tracking, which reverses the
mapping, so the identity of each connection lives in the kernel's table
rather than in the rule. Two consequences matter for the platform.
Established connections survive rule deletion, which lets the controller
bulk-delete a member's forward rules when the member leaves the pool and
lets new connections re-select while established ones finish. And because
the kernel keeps connections apart by their full five-tuple, the rule can
be scoped to the client or, as in the reported configuration, to a single
connection of it, so a pooled client spreads its connections across
members. Each domain tracks its connections in its own zone, and rules
that belong to a domain share a cookie so they can be removed together.

The load is exerted by a scheduled driver that runs on the host beside
the testbed. Each client is a Linux network namespace attached to its
domain's client network, and the driver issues requests from a namespace
as HTTP calls against the domain's virtual service address, so the
controller observes the namespace's source address and steers each
request through the data path above. A run instantiates 24 clients per
domain, 48 in total. The driver executes the run's phase schedule
(Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}): each phase fixes its duration, a
per-client issuing rate, the fraction of clients that are active, and its
request mix. Active clients issue requests continuously at the phase rate
against users and content items taken from a snapshot of the seeded data
set, so traffic generation does not depend on a live database, and the
run's seed fixes which clients are active and the per-client request
streams. 

The delivery of the load is open-loop, so a client keeps issuing
its requests on schedule while earlier requests are still outstanding and
the offered demand is preserved when the service slows down. Three
client-side bounds make the resulting failure modes explicit and finite.
Each client keeps at most 1024 requests in flight, and a request that
arrives when the window is full is recorded as dropped. Every request
carries a 300~s client-side cap, and a request that reaches it is
recorded as a timeout. In-flight requests that outlast the 30~s phase-end
drain are abandoned and recorded as cancelled. Each issued request is
written to the run's request record with its phase, kind, target
identifiers, issue time, outcome class, and latency, and the record,
together with the phase snapshot captured per run, is the raw material of
the per-run analyses of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\section{Distributed Elastic Allocation of Resources}\label{sec:impl_elastic}

\subsection{Compute}\label{sec:impl_compute}

A compute replica is a Flask application served by the Werkzeug server.
It exposes the workload routes, a drain endpoint that moves the replica
into a quiescing state, and a readiness endpoint. The readiness predicate
is a real service round-trip, and the endpoint answers 200 only while a
MongoDB ping against the domain's virtual data address succeeds. The
server binds before readiness is declared, so the predicate means
servability and the readiness claim cannot precede the socket. After each
request the replica pushes a per-request telemetry record, and with the
readiness event enabled it emits an \texttt{app\_ready} control event
when the predicate first succeeds.

The controller provisions a replica by starting its container with the
tier's quotas and the run's environment, attaching it to the domain
network through an OVS port, and recording it as a backend of the domain.
Removal follows the two phases of Section~\ref{sec:elastic_allocation}.
The replica is first withdrawn from the routing pool and signalled to
drain, and the drain completes once its in-flight requests reach zero and
a quiet period passes. Only then is its network attachment removed.

Readiness admission is implemented by a component that holds newly
spawned replicas in a pending registry and admits them to the routing
pool only when its configured rule is satisfied. The rule is selected by
a propagation mode.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{off}. The replica is admitted as soon as it is
    provisioned, and no readiness rule applies.
    \item \texttt{direct}. The replica is admitted on the
    \texttt{app\_ready} event itself, with no probe before admission. A
    fallback wait bounds how long the gate waits for the event before it
    probes anyway, so a lost notification has a recovery path.
    \item \texttt{discovery}. The event is ignored and the controller
    probes the readiness endpoint on a fixed poll cadence, admitting on
    the first successful probe.
    \item Wake-and-verify, a sub-mode of the direct rule. The event wakes
    a probe and admission requires the probe to pass, and the fallback
    wait applies when no event arrives.
\end{itemize}

% FIGURE: readiness-gate decision flow. A readiness claim from a pending
% replica on the left, the rule branches in the middle (direct admission,
% the fallback probe after the event wait, discovery polling, and
% wake-and-verify), and admission into the routing pool or abandonment on
% the right. Label each admitted edge with the source recorded in the
% admission log.

Probes retry on a fixed cadence with their own timeout. A replica that is
not admitted within the abandonment bound is abandoned and fully torn
down. Every admission records its source in the admission log, the event,
a poll, a fallback probe, or a wake-and-verify probe. A probe that
disagrees with an event-driven admission is recorded as a warning and the
admission stands, because the event already admitted the replica. The
wake-and-verify rule never admits unverified. The admission decision is
summarised in Algorithm~\ref{alg:readiness_admission}.

% Algorithm floats follow the chapter-indexed numbering used for tables and
% figures (the counter setup lives here rather than in the preamble).
\numberwithin{algorithm}{chapter}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{Readiness admission of a spawned compute replica.}
    \label{alg:readiness_admission}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \State enqueue the replica in the pending registry
        \If{mode is \texttt{off}}
            \State admit the replica
        \ElsIf{mode is \texttt{discovery}}
            \State probe \texttt{/ready} on each poll and admit on the first success
        \Else \Comment{\texttt{direct}}
            \State admit the replica on the \texttt{app\_ready} event
            \State without an event after the fallback wait: probe and admit on success
            \State with wake-and-verify: the event wakes a probe, and admission requires it to pass
        \EndIf
        \State abandon and tear down a replica not admitted within the abandonment bound
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Warm-lease priority and slow-start ramps are separate selection
behaviours of the routing path (Section~\ref{sec:vip_data_paths}) and
are held constant in the readiness experiments.

\subsection{Storage}\label{sec:impl_data}

Each domain's storage is a MongoDB replica set of its own, built in three
steps. At network build time, the domain's storage container starts as
the set's single member and becomes its primary, and the run's data set
is seeded through it. At runtime, a new member starts as a bare
\texttt{mongod} with a companion sidecar. The sidecar connects to a known
member of the set, appends itself as a non-voting member with zero
priority so it cannot affect elections, and applies the change with a
single \texttt{replSetReconfig} call. When the member reaches the
secondary state and can serve reads, the sidecar emits the
\texttt{rs\_secondary\_ready} control event, and the controller admits
the member into the domain's virtual data address, by default on the
event, with a telemetry-observed secondary state as the fallback path.
Removal reverses the steps. The member leaves the routing pool first, and
the controller then performs \texttt{rs.remove()} against the primary,
waits for the removal to be confirmed, and tears the container down.
The routing surface is the domain's virtual data address, and the 
edge server's database driver never learns the physical membership.

When the persistent reserve is enabled, one readied member is prepared
ahead of demand and held outside the routing pool. The storage scale-up
is then the activation of this prepared reserve, a membership that is
already joined and servable, so the reaction path skips the join and its
wait, and a replacement reserve is prepared once the slot is free. The
member lifecycle is bounded by the run's caps
(Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}).

Reads are issued against the domain's virtual data address through
per-request leases that the edge server manages per domain epoch. When
the address's membership changes, the epoch rotates and pooled
connections are retried with backoff, and reads prefer a secondary.
Writes are issued directly to the replica-set primary, so the
consistency contract is primary writes with eventually consistent
secondary reads. Selective synchronisation (Tier~1), which holds a
partial copy of the data in the consumer domain, and cross-domain
storage placement, which places a replica-set member in the peer
domain, are capabilities of Section~\ref{sec:elastic_data} and are
disabled in the reported experiments.

\section{Monitoring and Decision Engine}\label{sec:impl_monitoring}

Observation begins at the producers. Each compute replica emits a
per-request record of its resource state and request experience, and each
storage member's sidecar emits replication and resource-state samples as
the member reports activity, with a periodic heartbeat that keeps an idle
node visible. A bootstrap heartbeat makes a starting node visible before
its first request. A per-domain aggregator collects these records and
publishes a windowed summary at a fixed cadence. Every window is
published, including empty ones, and each summary carries the time of
each node's last report. Each window also carries a producer-side
overload label computed from fixed thresholds that are identical across
the compared runs.

The delivery of summaries to the controller is the configurable interface
of Section~\ref{sec:monitoring_decision}, implemented as alternative
sources over the same published windows. Every published window is
appended to a durable, sequence-numbered log, and the numbering continues
across aggregator restarts, because the aggregator reloads the log's tail
at boot. A mid-run controller restart re-pulls the log and may re-deliver
windows, which the analysis deduplicates by window identity.

with its network, sequence, window end, delivery time, delay, and source
mode, and this record is what the delivery experiments of

on the push path, regardless of the telemetry source in use.

On the measurement side, a collector writes per-run resource records for
each domain, debug records, and per-node files, and samples the CPU and
memory of both controller instances. The controller's own records
complete the timing evidence: the lifecycle of each provisioned unit,
the elasticity events, the admission log of the readiness gate, and the
decision log of the selection gate, all kept per run beside the request
record of Section~\ref{sec:impl_infrastructure} and the delivery log
above. Inside the controller, the delivered
windows feed the routing state and the decision engine of
Section~\ref{sec:monitoring_decision}. The engine evaluates each
delivered window with the policy of Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}, and
every decision is written to the decision log with its evidence. In the
bottleneck-aware configuration, the selection gate commits at most one
action per window and records the bottleneck class it assigned, the
per-tier evidence, the selected and rejected actions, and the budget
counters. Lifecycle signals, such as the completion of a drain or the
readiness of a member, reach the same decision path, and admission or
release follows the same flow as the actions themselves. Absence
detection and scale-down evaluation run on a fixed ticker once per
delivered window rather than on window arrival. The configuration surface
is environment-driven, and its values are listed in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}.

\section{Control Workflow}\label{sec:impl_control}

Each domain runs its own controller instance. The three execution
contexts of Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow} are implemented as one
cooperative event loop and two native threads. The packet path handles
flow decisions inside the OpenFlow event loop and never blocks on
infrastructure work. The telemetry consumer is a greenthread on the same
loop that receives the published summaries, updates the per-backend and
per-member state, evaluates the tier conditions, and posts typed alerts
to a priority queue. The elasticity thread is a daemon thread that blocks
on that queue and performs the infrastructure changes, and the readiness
gate runs a probe worker thread of its own.

State crosses the contexts through the shared structures of
Section~\ref{sec:control_workflow}. The telemetry consumer writes the
per-node state and the alerts between its yield points, the elasticity
thread hands its results to the routing pools through the same
registration the packet path reads, and the packet path reads the pools
on each flow decision. Alerts are queued, so a slow infrastructure change
does not stall request steering.

On the first packet of a new connection, no flow rule matches and the
packet is sent to the controller. The controller selects a unit with the
cost function of Section~\ref{sec:architecture_overview}, installs the
rules described in Section~\ref{sec:impl_infrastructure}, and sends the
packet on. Subsequent packets follow the installed rules. The rules carry
a configurable idle timeout, which resets as the connection stays
active, and a configurable hard timeout, after which the rule expires and
the next packet triggers a fresh selection. The evaluated values are
listed per campaign in Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}. When a unit
leaves a pool, its rules are removed, and on the
data path established connections continue through the kernel's
connection tracking while new connections re-select.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:first_packet} summarises the first-packet path.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{First-packet handling for a new connection.}
    \label{alg:first_packet}
    \begin{algorithmic}[1]
        \State the first packet of a new connection finds no flow rule
        \State send the packet to the controller
        \State select a unit from the eligible pool with the cost function
        \State install the routing rule for the client and the unit
        (Section~\ref{sec:impl_infrastructure})
        \State send the packet on, and later packets follow the rule
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A request waits ahead of the edge service in three places, which the
instrumentation separates. The first is the connection setup through the
virtual service address. The second is the first packet's round trip to
the controller and the installation of its rules. The third is the queue
on the selected replica before the application reads the request. The
first two are part of the steering path described here, and the third
accrues on the serving side. The waiting time is read as a difference,
user-visible latency minus the reported edge service time.

The controller keeps its state in memory. The routing pools, the
per-node telemetry, and the lifecycle records of in-flight operations
are not written durably, so a restart begins from an empty state and
re-derives what it needs from the network: the topology worker
re-observes the switches and rebuilds the hop cache and the routing
pools, the telemetry resumes and repopulates the per-node state, and
units without fresh reports receive the configured default in
selection. On the switch connection, the stale flows are flushed and the
catch-all rule is reinstalled, so later connections are selected again.
Because a restart resets this state, the evaluated runs exclude mid-run
controller restarts.

request mix, which determines whether an episode binds the compute tier or the
storage tier. The spatial axis, that is, which domain owns the requested
content, is held constant in the evaluation, as described above.

every run stores the phase schedule it executed.

baseline window opens every run at a low, steady load (rate 1.0 per client,

0.5, 5~\% of clients) and acts as a post-episode quiet period that lets the platform
settle and separates the stressed window from the post-shift windows. 
A demand drop returns the load to a low, steady level (rate 1.0, 10~\%) and is
the release window, in which the capacity the episode required is scaled
down and post-shift service quality is compared with the baseline. An idle
tail closes the run at a near-idle load (rate 0.05, 5~\%), so that every
configuration reaches sustained idleness and the release of surplus
capacity can complete. Table~\ref{tab:campaign_workloads} summarises

\subsection{Metrics and Outcome Definitions}\label{sec:metrics_definitions}
For the telemetry delivery campaign, the primary reaction outcome is
usable-capacity latency: the time from the onset of the demand episode to the
first new capacity serving traffic, reconstructed from the recorded timeline.
The time to the first scaling decision is reported as a descriptive outcome
only, because the delivery treatment determines when the evidence that
triggers a decision reaches the controller, and a decision cannot be taken
before that evidence arrives.

    For the scaling action campaign, the outcomes are read per arm, each
arm being one scaling policy under one demand regime: the targeted-tier
relief, as the change in the receiving tier's utilisation and latency
between the pre-registered windows before and after the action, and the
service recovery, as the episode service quality against the no-action
comparator, with the resource use reported alongside. Saturation, as this
campaign evaluates it, is the capacity-bound state in which a tier runs at
its capacity limit, arriving requests queue and completion slows, rather
than the state of an overloaded server whose own processing slows.

    The scaling action results use the request-path latency quantities
defined in Section~\ref{sec:vip_data_paths}: user-visible latency, edge
service time, its data-access and edge-processing components, and the
pre-dispatch wait. The edge service components are aggregated as
per-request means over the demand episode. The readiness admission campaign 
reports the segment of the chain from the scaling decision to usable capacity, 
which isolates the admission path.

Service quality is summarised per run through percentiles (p50, p95) of
the endpoint latency over the demand episode. Latency percentiles are computed
on the served basis, over completed requests. Outcome classes are reported
distinctly as offered requests, completed requests, failed
requests, timeouts (each recorded at the client-side cap of 300~s), and
client-side cancellations and drops at phase end.
Completion is reported on the served basis, completed requests over
non-abandoned requests, and on the offered basis, completed requests over
offered requests. Unless stated otherwise, each run contributes the mean of
its two domains, and any cross-domain asymmetry above the pre-registered
symmetry screen is reported.

The outcomes each campaign reports follow the facets of its research
question.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{The telemetry delivery campaign} answers the observation 
    part of its question directly: the delivered fraction states 
    how much of the produced window stream reached the decision engine, 
    and the information age at decision states how fresh the evidence 
    it received was. How the controller responded is read on its primary
    reaction outcome, the usable-capacity latency, and what users 
    experienced is read on the episode latency percentiles, both defined
    at the start of this subsection.
    \item \textit{The scaling action campaign} answers its question along its three
    axes, per arm: targeted-tier relief, as the relief obtained in the tier
    that received the action; service recovery, as the episode service
    quality against the no-action comparator; and resource use, as the
    scaling actions taken and the compute and storage node-minutes of the
    units those actions provision. A node-minute is one provisioned unit
    counted for each minute it exists, so the totals characterise how much
    capacity-time a policy keeps in service beyond the static fleet, not
    how busy that capacity is. One unit kept for thirty minutes and three
    units kept for ten minutes both total thirty node-minutes. The totals
    are normalised per thousand recorded requests. The load spread across the replicas of the
    receiving tier, as each replica's share of the served demand and its
    utilisation, reports the mechanism through which the action executed.
    \item \textit{The readiness admission campaign} answers its question in three parts.
    When the new backend serves is analysed along the admission chain: the
    intervals from verified readiness to admission, from admission to the
    first routed request, and from provisioning to the first successful
    response, and the relief of the existing fleet once the new backend
    begins serving. How much a lost notification costs is measured under
    readiness-event loss: the slow-share, the fraction of served requests
    that time out or exceed one second, over the full plateau (the
    campaign's demand episode), the onset window (the first 150~s of the
    plateau), and the plateau tail (the last 300~s of the plateau); and the
    number of admissions, the candidates abandoned and
    torn down. How much a
    premature claim costs is read as the change in the same user-visible
    measure against a matched no-fault run. A backend counts
    as admitted when it becomes visible in the routing pool, not
    when its registration is recorded.
\end{itemize}

Control overhead is reported as the CPU utilisation and resident memory of the
controller processes, and as the volume of control records, along each
campaign.

\subsection{Campaign Designs and Analysis}\label{sec:campaign_analysis}
All three campaigns follow the same discipline of single-variable
manipulation. Each campaign varies exactly one interface of the
demand-to-capacity chain and holds the other two constant, so that the
measured differences are attributable to the interface under test. The
policies compared within a campaign are baselines that encode architectural
properties, such as a fixed single-tier scaling policy or a periodic discovery
mechanism, and not competing aspects. Each campaign was pre-registered with the
treatments, primary outcomes, screening rules, and analysis tests all fixed
before data collection. 

No results are pooled across campaigns. Every contrast
is computed within its own campaign's design, and where a research question
draws on more than one campaign the campaigns are reported separately.

    Each campaign organises its runs so that every planned contrast is measured
over matched demand. A run is assigned a seed that fixes the randomised
content of its demand: which clients are active in each phase and, for each
client, the sequence of request kinds it issues, the users and content
items it targets, the payload values it sends, and the jitter on its
request timing. One integer produces all of this. The seed initialises
the driver's random process, the active-client sets are drawn from it,
and each client's stream continues from the seed offset by the client's
index. Runs that share a seed are therefore exposed to the same
demand by construction, given the campaign's phase profile and client set.
The runs of each campaign are organised into blocks: a block contains one
run of every treatment the campaign compares, its arms, and
the runs of a block share one seed, so that the treatments entering a
contrast are measured under identical demand. The seed values are labels
for independent demand realisations, not derived quantities. Each
campaign fixes its own range before data collection, and no seed is
reused within a campaign.
The order in which a block's treatments are executed is counterbalanced
across blocks, so that slow drift in the testbed over the campaign cannot
align with a single treatment.

    The number of replicates per campaign was fixed before data collection so
that a fully separated contrast remains resolvable under the pre-registered
test. Under complete separation between two treatment sets of $n$ runs, the
exact Mann--Whitney $U$ test has a $p$-value floor of $2/\binom{2n}{n}$:
0.0022 at six per treatment and 0.0006 at seven. The exact test shows
that six and seven runs per arm are enough to discount run-to-run
variation as the source of the observed difference. The campaigns use these
sizes: six per arm in the scaling action and readiness admission campaigns,
with the readiness-admission relief contrast drawn from a separate set at seven
runs per arm, and seven per arm in the telemetry delivery campaign. That campaign
takes an extra replicate because its runs showed a real run-to-run spread even
when repeated under the same configuration and seed.

    The reported contrasts follow the test pre-registered for each campaign.
Two of the three campaigns evaluate their contrasts with the exact
Mann--Whitney $U$ test. The test compares two sets of runs by ranking their
values together and counting how often a value from one set exceeds a value
from the other, so it establishes whether the two treatments differ without
assuming that the measured outcomes follow any particular distribution. The
exact form considers every way the observed values could be split into two
treatment sets of the given sizes and reports the probability of a
separation at least as strong as the observed one when the treatments are
in fact equivalent. It is used because the replicate counts are small, six
or seven runs per arm, which makes the complete enumeration feasible and
avoids the normal approximation used for larger samples. Alongside each
exact $p$-value, the campaigns report an effect size, Cliff's $\delta$,
computed as the probability that a value from one treatment exceeds a value
from the other minus the reverse probability. An effect size is reported
because significance alone does not show how separated the runs are. The
value of $\delta$ runs from $-1$ to $1$. A negative value means the runs
of the first treatment tend to be lower than those of the second, and a
positive value means they tend to be higher. At $-1$, every run of the
first treatment lies below every run of the second. At $1$, the order
reverses. The two extreme cases are called complete separation, and values
near $0$ mean the two treatments overlap. The closer the value to $-1$ or $1$, the more the difference is carried by the runs themselves, and a complete separation can only be preserved or weakened by additional replicates, never strengthened. Where a confidence
interval is reported, it is a 95~\% interval, which gives the range of
plausible values rather than a single number. This level suits the
replicate counts of six or seven runs per arm: a lower level would weaken
the guard against chance results, and a higher level would widen the
intervals enough that the pre-registered criteria could not be resolved.

In short, the two statistics answer different questions about the same
comparison. The exact test asks whether the difference between the arms is
larger than run-to-run variation could plausibly produce, and the effect
size asks how far apart the runs lie and in which direction. Together they
let a small set of matched runs support a conclusion, in place of
comparing averages, which a single atypical run could mislead.

Each campaign uses these tests and intervals on its own measures. The
telemetry delivery campaign reports each $\delta$ with a 95~\% bootstrap
confidence interval, and a direction count, the number of seed-matched
blocks consistent with the predicted order. The scaling action campaign
works with ratios instead of groups: for each arm it reports the median
ratio across runs, with a 95~\% confidence interval, and compares it with
the pre-registered threshold, because its question is whether a ratio
passes a target, not whether two sets of runs differ. The readiness
admission campaign reports how long each new backend takes from verified
readiness to admission, compared separately for fast-binding and
slow-binding backends, and reports its event-loss comparison as differences
in the slow share, in percentage points, within each matched block, and its
premature-claim comparison as per-rule damage against a matched no-fault
run. It
reports no confidence intervals, because its pre-registered checks were
thresholds and agreement across blocks. No correction is made for testing
several contrasts at once, because each campaign fixed a small number of
named contrasts in advance.

Screening rules are applied before the contrasts as runs
entering a contrast must pass the campaign's per-run checks on data-path
integrity, test-harness behaviour, and delivery fidelity; cross-domain
symmetry is screened, with breaches above the screening line reported as
flags rather than exclusions, and the defined exclusions are removed and
reported. Pre-registered criteria
that are not met are reported as such, together with the observed evidence and
its documented causes. The full design of each campaign, its treatments, 
held constants, workload, replication, exclusions, and pre-registered test, 
is summarised in a table at the start of its results section. The concrete
values of the parameters held constant are listed per campaign in
Appendix~\ref{app:campaign_config}.

\section{Telemetry Delivery Semantics (RQ1)}\label{sec:results_rq1_delivery}

The campaign compared the three delivery semantics named in
Section~\ref{sec:research_questions} under the overload episode described in
Section~\ref{sec:experimental_setup}, with the lossy class realised in
two forms (arms~C and~D).
Arm~A (event-preserving) hands every completed telemetry
window to the decision engine exactly once and in source order.
Arm~B (delayed event-preserving) hands the same ordered windows with a fixed,
pre-registered delay of 30~s and no burst replay.
Arm~C (latest-state polling) delivers only the most recent
completed window at each 30~s poll.
Arm~D (sampled push) delivers every third completed window immediately
and drops the intermediate windows.

    Each arm stands for a behaviour of the telemetry path 
as it can occur in practice. Arm~A is the best 
event-driven case where every completed window is 
delivered promptly and in order, losing nothing. 
Arm~B is the case of a path that carries a delay 
while losing nothing as every window still arrives, in
order, after a fixed lag, and the backlog is never replayed. 
Arm~C is the regular polling mode, the conventional monitoring 
practice reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit_monitoring} and here the 
consumer reads the current window at a fixed cadence, 
so everything produced between two reads goes unseen and
what is read can already be one interval old.
Arm~D is the case of a path that loses evidence without 
delaying it, most windows never arrive, and the ones that 
do are delivered promptly. The four arms change one property
of the path at a time: arm~B removes freshness from arm~A,
arm~D removes completeness from arm~A, and arm~C removes
both, so that the cost of late evidence is separated from the
cost of missing evidence. The pre-registered expectation was
the ordering A < B < C $\approx$ D on both the reaction 
and the per-episode service-quality outcomes.

    The aggregation window, scaling policy,
routing policy, and workload were held constant across arms. Each arm was run
seven times, once in each of seven seed-matched blocks, with the four arms
of a block sharing the block's seed and the seed changing between blocks
(seeds 3001--3007, giving 28 runs in total), so that the four treatments are
re-tested under a different demand realisation in each block. Each run is
treated as an experimental unit. Table~\ref{tab:rq1_design} summarises the
design of the campaign.

A post-campaign analysis of the run artefacts attributes the spread of the
delayed arm to the platform state each run samples rather than to the
decision engine. Across all seven delayed runs the decision engine
behaved consistently: the decision evidence carried the treatment's fixed
30~s lag, the first scale-up decision
fell between 52.0~s and 60.4~s, and usable capacity between 55.1~s and
61.5~s. The user-visible outcome depends on how much of the surge the base
fleet must absorb alone before the new capacity serves traffic.
That window is 25--33~s in A, 55--62~s in B, 74--82~s in C and 75--88~s in D. 

Whether that window is survived also depends on the testbed's run-to-run
variance, which is present in every arm and is not attributable to the
delivery treatment: the episode median compute processing time varied
roughly sixfold (16--94~ms) across runs of identical configuration. The
base fleet has only a limited absorption margin against the surge (the spare
capacity that absorbs requests until the new capacity serves), and a slow
platform state exhausts it sooner than a fast one. A's exposure window
is short enough that the margin covers it in every run, so the variance
leaves no visible trace in its outcome. The window of C and D is long
enough that the margin is exhausted in every run, so both arms degrade in
all seven replicates. B sits at the boundary: the margin covers its window
in five of the seven replicates, while in the two that sample a slow state
the margin runs out and the arm collapses to lossy-arm levels. The delivery
semantics set the exposure window, and the platform state decides whether
the base fleet's absorption margin covers it.

Two pre-registered expectations were not reproduced, and both are reported
as such. The full ordering A < B < C $\approx$ D did not hold, because B
does not sit reliably between A and C: its collapsed replicates straddle C
(B--C $p = 0.209$, direction 5/7). The sampled-push arm was also not strictly
worst: C and D were statistically indistinguishable ($p = 0.456$), with the
point estimate, if anything, marginally in the sampled-push arm's favour. Read together with the margin effect
described above, both deviations are informative rather than negative. The
collapsed delayed replicates show that the cost of delay is conditional on
the platform state that the arm samples, and the equivalence of C and D
shows that, once the intermediate windows are gone, whether the loss arises
from polling or from sampling leaves the cost unchanged. The expectation of
a single severity ordering across the two failure modes therefore did not
hold in full. What the campaign measured instead is two failure modes that
differ in kind, for one the loss of intermediate evidence acts robustly, 
while for the other delivery delay acts conditionally on the margin it 
consumes. One delayed replicate also showed a domain asymmetry above 
the campaign's threefold screening line (domain~2 p95 21.1~s against 
4.0~s in domain~1). It was the only run above that line.

The cost is confined to the overload episode. Outside it the arms were
indistinguishable, with baseline, recovery and demand-drop p95 at about
1.05--1.08~s in every arm (bar two single-window recovery blips, one in~C at
4.2~s and one in~D at 5.8~s), and no run recorded a single timeout inside the
episode. Cancellation and dropped shares were flat across arms at roughly 
5--7~\%, with the small completed-without-status class remaining largest
in B (2.22~\% against 0.03--0.42~\% elsewhere). A per-bucket check (with 
each bucket being chunks of 30s, within the episode from start to finish) 
confirmed that the episode ordering is not an artefact of aggregating
the episode into a single percentile. On the evaluated testbed, the
observation interface therefore shapes the transient response to a
demand shift, while leaving steady-state quality otherwise untouched.

In sum, the delivery semantics changed what the controller observed about 
the overload and when the response became usable capacity, with the lossy 
arms taking nearly three times as long as the reference. They also 
changed the quality of service users experienced, with an approximately 
ninefold penalty in the episode endpoint p95 where intermediate evidence 
was lost and a delay cost that scales with the absorption margin the 
exposure window consumes. Under this workload, the two failure modes of 
the observation interface were therefore distinguishable and not 
interchangeable: loss of intermediate demand evidence was the robust 
driver of user-visible cost, while delivery delay was costly in proportion
to the margin it consumes.

\section{Bottleneck-Aware Scaling Action (RQ2)}\label{sec:results_rq2_action}

The campaign compared three scaling-action policies under the same demand
shifts: a fixed compute-only policy, a fixed storage-only policy, and
bottleneck-aware selection, each evaluated on a compute-bound episode
(600~s of pure service pressure) and a data-access-bound episode (480~s,
90~\% content lookups). Each episode is named after the tier its load
presses on: the compute tier in the first, the storage member in the
second. Whether the named tier actually becomes capacity-bound
(Section~\ref{sec:metrics_definitions}) is itself one of the results.
The six-arm campaign contributes six replicates
per arm (five seed-matched and one cross-seed; 34 of 36 runs valid, with
two runs excluded as documented container memory-cap incidents), and a
comparative extension contributes the element the original design
lacked: no-action comparators (a policy that scales nothing), against
which the consequence of any action can be measured. Comparisons that
draw on both campaigns carry labels recording the memory cap and software
build of each side.

Each policy stands for an operator choice about which tier the action
serves: scale only the tier that runs the requests, scale only the tier
that stores and serves the data, or scale the tier the episode presses on.
That tier is the bottleneck and bottleneck-aware selection reads the
per-tier telemetry, classifies the episode as compute-bound or
data-access-bound, and scales the matching tier, while each fixed policy
acts on its single tier alone.

The pre-registered expectation was that the aligned action would relieve
the tier that bound the episode and improve the user-visible service
against inaction and that the mismatched action would harm service relative
to inaction.

The relief check asked whether the aligned action relieved the tier that
bound the episode (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2_relief}). Where the storage tier
bound the episode, it did: the prepared reserve was activated in every
run where the storage action was selected (11 of 11) and shared the reads,
and the storage-member peak CPU fell to 0.57--0.66 of its pre-activation
level in all twelve storage-only domains.
The bottleneck-aware arm followed the same direction with eight of its
ten domains below the threshold. On the compute-bound episode no relief is reported because the compute
tier never entered the capacity-bound state, so there was no queue to
relieve. The action did spread the load across the added backends, with
no user-visible change (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2_engagement}).

The data-access reading follows from where the queue builds: with no
action the serving storage member carries the full read load alone, the
matched action moves a share of the reads to the reserve and cuts the
data-access component of the wait (about 79~ms to 52~ms), and the
mismatched action never reaches the data path. The data-access component
and the edge-processing component are the per-tier latency signals the
selection observes (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_policy}).

The scaling action on the compute-bound episode was executed in full
(Figure~\ref{fig:rq2_engagement}): the added replicas took most of each
run's requests (medians of about 71 and 72~\% in the compute-only and
bottleneck-aware arms), and the total compute spent rose to about four
times the single-backend load. The domain's traffic alternated between
members rather than loading them at once: in the windows a backend
carried traffic it served the full episode rate at about the
single-backend CPU (mostly 55--61~\% against 61~\%), so the all-window
medians in the panel (46--56~\%) sit below that level only through the
idle windows each member also has.

Resource use separates the policies where service does not: on the
compute-bound episode both compute-scaling arms spent compute without
changing the service, and on the data-access-bound episode the storage
action's spend became tier relief and user-visible benefit, while the
mismatched compute action's spend relieved nothing. In the evaluated
envelope, what reached users was decided by the tier a policy served, not by
the size of its spend.

Selection commitment holds across regimes. The bottleneck-aware classifier
committed to the detected bottleneck in every evaluated regime: on the
data-access-bound episode it activated the storage reserve first and scaled
compute only after storage relief, and on the compute-bound episode it scaled
compute without activating storage. Classifier-versus-episode agreement was
88--93~\% on the data-access-bound episode. The compute units the arm adds after
storage relief follow from the condition (edge utilisation rose past its
threshold while every request is served through the edge), not from a
compute deficit.

The compute-bound episode shows no such mirror, because pure service pressure
holds the storage tier near idle, so no sustained storage request arises
after the compute action.

Degradation was bounded on both sides. On the data-access-bound episode
the mismatched action came in between the matched action and no action
(about 0.8~s at the p95 against about 0.6~s and 1.6~s): it helped less
than the matched action while still beating inaction, so the
pre-registered harm expectation was not met. On the compute-bound
episode the cost of acting is the late-episode tail of the scaling arms
(about 4.6~s and 1.6~s at the p95, against about 7~ms elsewhere), which
forms while a replica is prepared mid-episode.

One pre-registered criterion remained formally unmet
(Table~\ref{tab:rq2_criteria}), the storage tail-latency test. It asks
whether the request tail latency (p95) clearly improved once the reserve
was active, by comparing the window before the activation with the window
after it. The tail did come out lower after the action (a median ratio of
0.727), but not clearly enough for the test, as the confidence interval
around that ratio still includes 1.0, the value that means no change at
all, so the criterion counts as unmet. The documented cause is a
measurement asymmetry between the two pinned windows: the long
post-activation window always contains the sparse completed-request tail
above 10~s, which the short pre-activation window rarely does, inflating
its p95 independently of the action. The storage reading therefore rests
on the tier relief and on the labelled benefit over inaction, and the
unmet gate is carried as such. Together with the bounded degradation
above, the expectation set is closed: within the operating envelope, the
only regime in which an action improved user-visible service was the one
where the served tier actually bound.

In sum, the results support one conditional conclusion: the aligned action
relieved the tier that bound the episode and delivered the labelled
user-visible benefit over inaction, and the benefit is absent where no
capacity-bound queue forms, however fully the action executes. The
selector committed to the detected bottleneck in every evaluated regime,
while the campaign reports no add-attributable compute recovery and no
demonstrated advantage of bottleneck-aware selection over a matched fixed
policy. 

The reported effects hold for the evaluated two-domain testbed,
its workloads, and its resource limits. The platform behaviours
observed at those limits are carried to Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} as
limitations and future work.

\section{Readiness Admission to Traffic (RQ3)}\label{sec:results_rq3_readiness}

This section evaluates readiness admission, the step that turns a
newly provisioned backend into usable capacity, spanning how fast the
controller admits the backend into the routing pool for load distribution
while everything works, and what each rule does when the readiness claim
is lost or early.

All rules observe the same verified-readiness state
(Table~\ref{tab:rq3_design}), with the readiness criterion, the
backend-selection function, the workload, and the resource limits held
fixed within each compared set, leaving the admission rule as the only
variable.

The rules differ in the evidence they trust. Direct lifecycle
notification admits on the readiness message the backend sends, without
re-checking. The probe fallback variant waits 20~s for that message and
otherwise probes the backend. Periodic discovery ignores the message and
probes on a 10~s poll. Wake-and-verify lets the message wake a probe and
admits only if the probe passes. The probe is the readiness health check
the controller sends, and it passes only once the backend can serve. The
direct rule's 130~s fallback timer never fires, because the controller
abandons a backend that is not admitted within 120~s, so the rule
depends entirely on the message.

The pre-registered expectation was that the rules would separate exactly
where their evidence fails. First, while the readiness event is intact,
direct lifecycle notification should admit new backends earlier, with
that head start showing only in when the redistribution begins, not in
how the fleet behaves once admission occurs. Second, under total event
loss with reduced headroom, a lost event without a recovery path should
leave the new capacity ready but unused, at a decisive but bounded cost
to service quality, while both re-deriving rules recover and relief
completes. Third, a premature readiness claim should expose the trusting
rules while the waiting rules absorb it.
The claim can arrive before the backend can serve, and the rules were
measured under that failure, with damage reported in the same measure as
above, the change in the share of requests that fail, time out, or
exceed one second against a matched no-fault run.

With everything working, the difference between the rules is the one the
design implies. Direct lifecycle notification admits the backend the
moment its message arrives, while periodic discovery waits for its next
poll. The measured gap is complete, with a median of 0.001~s against
6.984~s from verified readiness to admission ($\delta = -1.000$, earlier in
every backend), with the full chain in Table~\ref{tab:rq3_delays} and
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3_admission_intervals}. The head start reached the
user end to end. Provisioning to the first successful response was about
six seconds shorter under direct notification (11.27~s against 17.70~s,
exact $p = 0.0005$), and relief reached the loaded tier about four
seconds sooner (scaling decision to usable capacity 2.17~s against
6.01~s, exact $p = 0.0022$). Once admitted, both rules served the first
request in about a second. The load then left the existing fleet by the
same amount under both rules (relief $p = 0.209$), so the rule changes
when the redistribution begins, not how much it moves. A pre-registered
poll-interval sensitivity arm (six runs at a 15~s cadence) showed the
delay scaling with the poll period: the admission interval 15.22~s
against 9.62~s at the 10~s cadence, and the time from the scaling
decision to usable capacity 16.97~s against 11.45~s, both rising by
about the added five seconds (descriptive, Cliff's $\delta = -0.444$).
No user-visible cost of the seconds-level delay appeared in any
tested healthy regime, and the interval stayed inside the fleet's spare
capacity as the margin that the event-loss part of the campaign
deliberately removes.

diverge in kind (Table~\ref{tab:rq3_fault} and

readiness again through their own probes. The fallback variant admitted
after its fallback window, about 21~s after provisioning, and periodic
discovery within one poll period, beginning about four seconds sooner
(medians 17.1~s against 21.4~s). In the premature-claim grid's loss runs
(seeds 6301--6303), periodic discovery also recovered through its poll,
landing

at a 10.6~\% plateau median under the pre-registered 25~\% ceiling. The
existing fleet kept serving the episode throughout, so what the failure
removed was the fleet's margin, not the service.

One qualifier bounds the event-loss reading. Its pre-registered
conjunction was not fully met, the shortfall confined to two marginal
no-fault window breaches in the health-floor count that do not touch the
contrast, with the block-4 exclusion resting on a separate pre-registered
control-tail check recorded in Table~\ref{tab:rq3_fault}.

Under a premature claim, the rules separated by what their evidence
can verify. A rule that admits on the claim sends traffic to a backend
that cannot yet accept it, and the damage scales with how early the
claim came.
In the direct lifecycle configuration it rose with 
the lead, from a median of $+4.1$~pp at two seconds to $+8.1$ and $+12.6$~pp at five
and ten, and the transport failures inside the claim window rose in the
same order (55--181 at two seconds, 865--997 at ten). The probe fallback
variant shared the exposure, because its backup path reacts to a claim
that never arrives, not to one that arrives early, and it admitted a
present but premature claim as true (medians $-0.8$, $+8.6$ and
$+13.3$~pp). Periodic discovery stayed at its no-fault level across the
whole grid, with no positive reading above $+0.12$~pp and no window
failures in any seed. Its probe cannot pass before the backend can
serve, so the timing of the claim has nothing to act on. The
wake-and-verify variant, which also admits only through its probe, held
the same flat line at ten seconds ($-0.3$ and $-3.4$~pp), at a
cost of $+0.2$ and $+3.1$~pp on the no-fault runs at those two seeds, the second
over the pre-registered $+2$~pp descriptive bound (a third seed measured
$+0.5$~pp, its damage contrast removed by the pre-registered exclusion).

The pre-registered expectations were met where they committed
(Table~\ref{tab:rq3_soundness} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq3_soundness_damage}).
Exposure followed what each rule's evidence verifies. The trusting rules
paid for the premature claim in proportion to its lead, while the rules
that probe absorbed it, with periodic discovery keeping no positive
damage above $+0.12$~pp and no recorded violations across the grid. The
crossover pattern appeared in the direction and shape it named,
with the crossing at the smallest tested lead in all three seeds and the
fallback variant's growth interval clearing the pre-registered threshold,
but the pre-registered growth gate was not passed at this replication,
so the crossing is carried descriptively and its confidence is limited
by the three-seed replication.

In conclusion, while
the readiness event was intact, the admission interval was bounded by the
discovery interval and passed without user-visible cost. When the event was
lost under reduced headroom, the lifecycle-only configuration paid a
bounded service-quality cost while both re-deriving rules recovered. And
when the claim arrived early, the rules separated by what their evidence
can see. A premature claim cost the trusting rules in proportion to its
lead while the waiting rules priced it at nothing. Readiness-admission
coordination is therefore at once a latency choice (about seven seconds
of added delay while everything works) and a reliability choice, deciding
whether a ready backend's capacity is delayed or lost when the
notification is lost, and whether a premature notification reaches
traffic before the backend can serve. Because admission fills the
routing pool, the same choice sets what the load distribution can rely
on when load has triggered the scaling. A lost event hides ready
capacity from the pool. A premature claim exposes capacity that cannot
yet absorb traffic. The reported effects hold for the evaluated
two-domain testbed, its workloads, and its resource limits.


\section{Discussion: Synthesis Across Interfaces}\label{sec:discussion}

This section reads the three campaigns together. The campaigns ran on the same
platform, used the same metric definitions and instrumentation, and followed
the same discipline of varying one interface while holding the others fixed.
Each campaign, however, drove its own workload under its own demand regime, so
the three sets of results are not pooled and no quantity is combined across
them, consistent with the commitment in Section~\ref{sec:lit_synthesis} that
no aggregate coordination cost is claimed. What can be compared is the pattern
each campaign revealed: which effects held for every replicate, which depended
on a condition the campaign varied or sampled, and which the campaign could
not establish. This classification is the comparative synthesis promised in
Section~\ref{sec:contributions}, stated per interface in
Table~\ref{tab:synthesis}, and it prepares the answers summarised in
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.

Read across the campaigns, the measured effects share a shape. They appear
at the edge of the operating envelope, where a margin is consumed, and not
in the steady state. The delivery arms were indistinguishable outside the
overload episode, and the cost of delay appeared only when the base fleet's
absorption margin ran out. The loss of evidence stretched the window in
which the fleet had to cope alone, and the margin was exhausted in every
replicate. The action changed user-visible quality only where the episode
built a capacity-bound queue in the served tier, and neither the compute
action nor its relief reached users where no queue formed. The admission
delay cost nothing while the fleet held spare capacity, and the lost
notification cost the fleet's margin under the reduced headroom the
campaign fixed. On the evaluated testbed, the three interfaces decide how a
demand shift is weathered, not how the service performs once the shift has
passed.

The campaigns also separate failure modes that monitoring and scaling are
often discussed as one. In the delivery interface, missing evidence and
late evidence are different faults. Missing windows cost about nine times
the episode tail, with complete separation in every replicate, while late
windows cost in proportion to the margin they consume. Delay was harmless
in five delayed replicates and decisive in the two that sampled a slow
platform state. In the action
interface, execution and benefit are separate. The compute action spread
the load, yet users saw no change because no
capacity-bound queue existed to drain, and the storage action delivered
benefit precisely where the queue existed. The action also carried a cost
of its own: the second-tier compute additions in the bottleneck-aware arm
coincided with that arm's worst timeout shares in two of the five valid
replicates, so provisioning is not invisible to users while an episode is
live. In the admission interface, latency and reliability are separate. The
faster mechanism saved about seven seconds while the fleet was healthy, and
the same mechanism lost the admission entirely when its single notification
was dropped, while the re-deriving rules turned the same loss into a
bounded delay, and a premature notification cost only the rules that
admitted on it.

The interfaces also differ in the control records they leave behind, but
not in the controller cost that carries them. Controller CPU stayed near
14~\% in the delivery episodes, near 11~\% across the admission plateau,
and within 2--22~\% across the action episodes depending on the regime (the
storage-only policy at the low end, the compute-adding policies at the high
end), with memory near 70~MB everywhere. The records differ as designed:
about 335 deliveries per run under complete delivery against about
105--114 under the lossy arms, probe records in place of events under
discovery admission, and eight or six compute scale-ups per run where the
compute policies acted (configuration medians in
Table~\ref{tab:app_config_control_cost}).

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature_review} left the three questions of
Table~\ref{tab:interface_gaps} open, and the campaigns are their
experimental form. Read back against those questions, the results answer
them within the reviewed scope. On delivery, the reviewed works do not
trace an individual delivery semantic through a stateful service's
scaling path, and the closest precedent varies freshness for a single
consumer. The campaign distinguishes the two ways evidence can be
incomplete: missing intermediate windows is the robust driver of
user-visible cost, while a complete but delayed path costs in proportion
to the absorption margin it consumes. On the action, the reviewed
auto-scaling works fix the action type or evaluate coordination in
composite. The campaign shows that user-visible value follows the tier
that binds the episode, and that classification had no demonstrated
advantage over a fixed policy matched to the regime in the operating
envelope, which bounds the expected value of choosing the action at
runtime. On admission, the reviewed placement work names readiness but
leaves the step to traffic outside its scope. The campaign measures that
step as a bounded delay while the fleet is healthy, and as an
all-or-nothing loss under a dropped notification without re-derivation,
which the re-deriving rules convert into a bounded delay. These
statements characterise, within the reviewed literature, the interfaces
that the reviewed works leave open. They do not assert that the
coordination gap is universal.

Three considerations follow for the design of services like the one
evaluated, all bounded to the evaluated testbed. First, when an evidence path must survive
overload transients, completeness is the property to protect: dropping
intermediate windows was the robust driver of user-visible cost, while a
complete but delayed path degraded only where the absorption margin was
thin. Second, a scaling action should be conditional on the tier that
actually binds the episode: full execution of an action on a tier that was
not the bottleneck spent node-minutes without changing the service, and the
provisioning work itself perturbed the tail in the bottleneck-aware arm. 
Third, readiness admission
is a reliability path as much as a latency path: a bounded re-derivation
cost a few seconds while the fleet was healthy, and preserved the
provisioned capacity when the primary notification was lost. On the
evaluation side, the campaigns show that these interfaces are exercised by
the transient and the margin.

These statements inherit the scope and qualifiers of the campaigns that
produced them: a two-domain testbed on a single host with an emulated
inter-domain link, a stateful edge service over MongoDB replica sets, and
the evaluated workload families and resource limits. Cross-domain
storage placement remained held-constant platform context throughout, and
its characterisation is deferred to future work. The limitations of the
evaluation are detailed in Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}.
